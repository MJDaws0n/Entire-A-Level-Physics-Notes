### Why Springs?

*Source slide 3.*

- In physics we model different physical processes.
- We can model the intermolecular forces in a crystal as springs.
- We know that there are not little springs in between the atoms and molecules but the system can be modelled mathematically as if there are springs there.
- This is a very powerful concept and hence why we teach you from GCSE onwards about these bloody springs!

### Loading and Unloading Curves

*Source slide 4.*

- Different materials respond differently to tensile (stretching) forces.
- As a force is applied the material will extend.
- Yet as the force is released the material may not return to its usual shape in the same way.
- This difference is characterised by a materials loading and unloading curves.
- For example, a plastic bag will act differently from a metal wire.

### A Metal Wire

*Source slide 5.*

- A metal wire will have the same loading and unloading curve if it isn’t extended beyond its elastic limit.
- However, if it is extended beyond its elastic limit the unloading curve will be parallel to the loading curve.
- This results in a permanent deformation in the material.

### Rubber

*Source slide 6.*

- Rubber bands do not obey Hooke’s law.
- A rubber band will return to its original shape yet the unloading and loading curves will be different.
- The loop formed is called a hysteresis loop.
- More [[Glossary#Work Done|work done]] is needed to stretch the spring than unloading.
- This is due to a change in thermal energy.

### Polythene

*Source slide 7.*

- Polythene is used in little strips to make plastic bags.
- Polythene does not obey Hooke’s law.
- The strips are easily stretched yet suffer plastic deformation with relatively little force.
- Plastic deformation isn’t necessarily bad as this is how we form steel sheets into different shapes.

## Equations

### Springs and elastic energy

$$F=kx$$

Hooke's law within the limit of proportionality.

$$E_{\mathrm{elastic}}=\frac{1}{2}Fx=\frac{1}{2}kx^2$$

Area under a linear force-extension graph.

$$k_{\mathrm{parallel}}=k_1+k_2+\cdots$$

Parallel springs have the same extension and their forces add.

$$\frac{1}{k_{\mathrm{series}}}=\frac{1}{k_1}+\frac{1}{k_2}+\cdots$$

Series springs carry the same force and their extensions add.

### Stress, strain and Young modulus

$$\sigma=\frac{F}{A}$$

Tensile stress.

$$\varepsilon=\frac{\Delta L}{L}$$

Tensile strain.

$$E=\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}$$

Young modulus in the linear elastic region.

See [[Formula Sheet]] for the complete course formula set.

## Method

1. Use extension, not total length.
2. Find \(k\) from the gradient of a force-extension graph in the linear region.
3. For a non-linear graph, elastic energy is the area under the curve.
4. Use the original cross-sectional area and original length unless told otherwise.
5. Determine Young modulus from the gradient of a stress-strain graph in its straight-line region.

## Common mistakes

- The limit of proportionality and elastic limit are related but not identical.
- Strain is dimensionless; stress and Young modulus have unit pascal.
