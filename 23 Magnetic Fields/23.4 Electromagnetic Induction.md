## Core notes

### 23.4 Electromagnetic Induction

*Source slide 1.*

- More massive particle have a larger radius.
- Faster moving particles have a larger radius.
- Stronger magnetic fields reduce the radius.
- Particles with greater charge have a smaller radius.

### Electromagnetic Induction

*Source slides 3–6.*

- Electromagnetic induction is a phenomenon which was investigated by Faraday in the 1800s.
- Electromagnetic induction is utilised in [[Glossary#Power|power]] stations and wind turbines to generate electricity.
- Electromagnetic induction produces an e.m.f. by relative motion between a conductor and a magnetic field.
- The e.m.f can be measured with a voltmeter in parallel.
- A permanent magnet produces magnetic field lines.
- A coiled conductor has a large number [[Glossary#Density|density]] of charge carriers.
- We move a magnet towards the coil of wire and an e.m.f is induced across the ends of the wire.

### Magnetic Flux Linkage

*Source slide 14.*

- Electromagnetic induction involves a coil of conductor.
- Increasing the number of coils increases the induced e.m.f.
- The coil can be imagined as having multiple cross-sections.
- This produces a quantity called [[Glossary#Magnetic Flux|magnetic flux]] linkage.

## Equations

### Electromagnetic induction

$$\Phi=BA\cos\theta$$

Magnetic flux; \(\theta\) is between field and area normal.

$$\mathcal{E}=-\frac{\Delta(N\Phi)}{\Delta t}$$

Faraday's and Lenz's laws.

See [[Formula Sheet]] for the complete course formula set.

## Method

1. Find the change in flux linkage \(N\Phi\), not just the final flux.
2. Use the minus sign/Lenz's law to state that the induced effect opposes the change causing it.
3. Increase EMF by increasing turns, field, area, or rate of change.

## Common mistakes

- Flux uses the angle to the normal; using the angle to the plane swaps sine and cosine.
