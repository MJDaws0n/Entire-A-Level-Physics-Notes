## Core notes

### Speakers and Potential Differences

*Source slide 5.*

- Mains powered speakers take a [[Glossary#Potential Difference|potential difference]] of 230 V in UK households.
- Remember potential difference is the [[Glossary#Energy|energy]] per unit charge.
- So the higher potential difference the more energy.
- Speakers, are one of many electrical appliances, which makes use of potential divider circuits.
- They allow us to ‘tap’ off various voltages and so control the volume of speakers.

### Kirchhoff’s Second Law

*Source slide 6.*

- We know from Kirchhoff’s second law that the sum of the potential difference drops must equal the sum of the EMF.
- We also know from Ohm’s law that the potential difference across a component depends upon the [[Glossary#Resistance|resistance]] of the component and the current following through it.
- We can use these facts to allow us to ‘tap off’ various voltages in a circuit.

### Potential Dividers

*Source slides 7–8.*

- When we have a circuit we can simply ‘tap off’ from a voltage that we desire.
- For example, if I want a voltage of 3 V I can ‘tap off’ from R2.
- We could tap off at R1 and get a voltage of 9 V if we desired also.
- We always label the top resistor R1

### Determining the Output Voltage

*Source slide 9.*

- We have two methods to solve for the output potential difference.
- Both have associated equations given to you on the equation sheet.

### Ratio of Resistances

*Source slide 10.*

- The potential difference across each resistor in a potential divider depends on their resistances.
- It requires energy to move a charge carrier through a component.
- The higher the resistance the more energy is required.
- We can use a gravitational energy analogy to explain electrical resistance.

## Equations

### Potential dividers and sensing circuits

$$V_{\mathrm{out}}=\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}V_{\mathrm{in}}$$

Output across \(R_2\) for an unloaded divider.

$$\frac{V_1}{V_2}=\frac{R_1}{R_2}$$

Voltage ratio in a series pair.

### Resistors in series and parallel

$$R_{\mathrm{series}}=R_1+R_2+\cdots$$

Series combination.

$$\frac{1}{R_{\mathrm{parallel}}}=\frac{1}{R_1}+\frac{1}{R_2}+\cdots$$

Parallel combination.

See [[Formula Sheet]] for the complete course formula set.

## Method

1. Mark exactly which component the output voltage is measured across.
2. Work out how the sensor resistance changes, then how its share of the supply voltage changes.
3. If a load is connected, combine it in parallel with the relevant divider resistor before using the ratio.
4. Simplify one clear series or parallel group at a time.
5. Redraw the circuit after each simplification.
6. For parallel branches, the equivalent resistance is smaller than the smallest branch resistance.

## Common mistakes

- For an NTC thermistor, resistance falls as temperature rises; for an LDR, resistance falls as light intensity rises.
- Components are in series only if the same current must pass through them with no junction between.
