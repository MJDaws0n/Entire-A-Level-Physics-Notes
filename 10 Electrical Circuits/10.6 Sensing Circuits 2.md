## Core notes

### Potential Dividers Circuits

*Source slide 3.*

- When looking at potential dividers we have just presumed that we use fixed resistors.
- It is possible to change these components to differing types of variable resistors.

### Resistance and P.D

*Source slide 4.*

- The [[Glossary#Potential Difference|potential difference]] across each resistor in a potential divider depends on their resistances.
- The greater the [[Glossary#Resistance|resistance]] the more potential difference is ‘provided’ to that component.
- So more [[Glossary#Energy|energy]] is dissipated across that component.
- We can use a gravitational energy analogy to explain electrical resistance.

### Gravitational Analogy

*Source slide 5.*

- Let’s use an analogy between gravitational and electrical systems.
- When you are on the top of a larger hill you have a greater [[Glossary#Gravitational Potential|gravitational potential]].
- A greater resistance means there is a greater potential difference.

### LDR

*Source slide 6.*

- A LDR’s resistance depends upon light level (lux).
- As light level increases the resistance decreases.
- Light level and resistance are inversely proportional.

### LDR Sensing Circuit

*Source slide 8.*

- We can use a LDR in a circuit to control lighting, for example a security light.
- As the light level increases the resistance of the LDR decreases.
- As the resistance decreases the potential difference across the LDR decreases.

## Equations

### Potential dividers and sensing circuits

$$V_{\mathrm{out}}=\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}V_{\mathrm{in}}$$

Output across \(R_2\) for an unloaded divider.

$$\frac{V_1}{V_2}=\frac{R_1}{R_2}$$

Voltage ratio in a series pair.

### Current-voltage characteristics

$$R_{\mathrm{static}}=\frac{V}{I}$$

Resistance at one point on a non-linear characteristic.

$$r_{\mathrm{dynamic}}=\frac{\Delta V}{\Delta I}$$

Local/dynamic resistance from a tangent where required.

See [[Formula Sheet]] for the complete course formula set.

## Method

1. Mark exactly which component the output voltage is measured across.
2. Work out how the sensor resistance changes, then how its share of the supply voltage changes.
3. If a load is connected, combine it in parallel with the relevant divider resistor before using the ratio.
4. Identify the component and note whether its characteristic is linear and symmetric.
5. For a filament lamp, increasing temperature raises resistance.
6. A diode conducts strongly only in its forward direction after its characteristic begins to rise.

## Common mistakes

- For an NTC thermistor, resistance falls as temperature rises; for an LDR, resistance falls as light intensity rises.
- A curved \(I\)-\(V\) graph does not have one constant resistance.
