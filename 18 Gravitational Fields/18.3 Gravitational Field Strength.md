## Core notes

### Gravitational Field Strength

*Source slides 3–4.*

- We can combine our equation for [[Glossary#Gravitational Field Strength|gravitational field strength]] and Newton’s law of gravitation to yield a new relationship for gravitational field strength, g.
- The negative signs informs us that the field lines is the opposite to [[Glossary#Displacement|displacement]] r and so are attractive.
- Therefore, we gain two important relationships.
- Gravitational field strength, g, is proportional to the mass, M, of the object creating the field.
- Gravitational field strength, g, is inversely proportional to the square of the radius.

### Graphical Representation

*Source slide 6.*

- A graph of gravitational field strength against distance from the centre of the mass will show a 1/r2 relationship.
- The gradient would be equal to –GM.

### Uniformed Fields

*Source slide 7.*

- As you may have noticed the gravitational field strength at the ISS is still 88 % of the value on the surface of earth.
- Therefore, we presume that on Earth the value of g is the same throughout Earth including at the top of the greatest mountain.
- We say that the gravitational field on earth is uniformed.

## Equations

### Gravitational fields

$$g=\frac{F}{m}$$

Gravitational field strength: force per unit mass.

### Radial gravitational field strength

$$g=\frac{GM}{r^2}$$

Magnitude outside a spherical mass.

See [[Formula Sheet]] for the complete course formula set.

## Method

1. Use field lines to show the direction of force on a small test mass.
2. Closer field-line spacing represents a stronger field.
3. Measure \(r\) from the centre of the source mass.
4. Use \(g\) in \(\mathrm{N\,kg^{-1}}\), equivalent to \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\).

## Common mistakes

- Gravitational potential is energy per unit mass and is scalar; field strength is force per unit mass and is vector.
- The equation \(g=GM/r^2\) applies outside a spherically symmetric mass.
