### Johannes Kepler

*Source slide 3.*

- Kepler was a German astronomer and mathematician who in the 17th century published three laws on planetary motion.
- These laws were based solely upon observation.
- Kepler had no understanding of gravitational fields as it was his work which helped Newton formulate his laws.

### Kepler’s First Law

*Source slide 4.*

- 1) The orbit of a planet is an ellipse with the sun at one of the two foci (positions).
- An ellipse is a ‘squashed’ circle which has been elongated.
- Eccentricity is the measure of the ‘squashed-ness’ of the circle.
- High eccentricity are very squashed circles.
- Low eccentricity ellipses can be modelled as circles.

### Kepler’s Second Law

*Source slides 7–8.*

- 2) A line segment joining a planet and the sun sweeps out equal areas in equal time.
- When planets move in an elliptical orbit around the sun their speed is not constant.
- Planets closer to the sun move faster.

### Kepler’s Third Law

*Source slide 10.*

- 3) The square of the orbital period, T, of a planet is directly proportional to the cube of the average distance, r, from the Sun.
- As orbital period, T, and the average distance, r, are directly proportional we can introduce a constant of proportionality, k.

### Kepler arrived at the third law empirically.

*Source slide 11.*

- He had data from the planets in the solar system.
- He looked at this data and found the relationship that he would state in his third law.
- Kepler wrote: “I first believed I was dreaming...
- “But it is absolutely certain and exact that the ratio which exists between the period times of any two planets is precisely the ratio of the 3/2th [[Glossary#Power|power]] of the mean distance.”
- The mean distance between the earth and the sun is known as one astronomical unit, AU.

### Plotting Data

*Source slide 13.*

- From Kepler’s third law we know that the square of the orbital period, T, of a planet is directly proportional to the cube of the average distance, r, from the Sun.
- When we plot T2 against r3 we expect a straight line
- through the origin with the constant, k, being the gradient.

### Planetary Motion

*Source slide 16.*

- Planets have a low eccentricity orbits.
- Orbits of planets in the solar systems can be modelled as circles.
- Therefore, we can use our knowledge of circular motion (Chapter 16).
- We can then link this with Newton’s law of gravitation (Lesson 18.2) to find an expression for the constant of proportionality between T2 against r3

## Equations

### Kepler's laws and circular orbits

$$T^2=\frac{4\pi^2}{GM}r^3$$

Circular orbit/Kepler's third law.

$$v=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}$$

Speed in a circular gravitational orbit.

See [[Formula Sheet]] for the complete course formula set.

## Method

1. For a circular orbit, set gravitational force equal to the required centripetal force.
2. Use orbital radius from the central body's centre, including its radius when altitude is given.

## Common mistakes

- A satellite in circular orbit is continuously falling; it is not outside the gravitational field.
