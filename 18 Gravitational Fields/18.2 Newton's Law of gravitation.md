## Core notes

### 18.2 Newton’s Law of Gravitation

*Source slide 1.*

- As distance from the planets surface increases the [[Glossary#Density|density]] of field lines decreases.
- As field lines are lines of [[Glossary#Force|force]] the gravitational force decreases with distance.

### Density of Earth

*Source slides 3–4.*

- In 1774, the astronomer Nevil Maskelyne set out to determine the density of Earth with the help of mathematician, Charles Hutton.
- They used a pendulum and the Scottish mountain, Schiehallion, to determine the density.
- Through utilising the deflection caused by the gravitational field of the mountain they managed to determine the density of earth with an error of only 20% off the modern accepted value.
- This calculation allowed us to estimate the density and thus mass of planets in our solar system and also the sun’s mass.
- This experiment ultimately allowed us to make good estimates of the forces experienced by the planets in their orbits through using Newton’s law of gravitation.

### Newton’s Law of Gravitation

*Source slides 5–6.*

- Newton’s law of gravitation is a relationship between objects with the property of mass and the forces caused by that property.
- It says that force and masses of the objects are proportional.
- It also states that the gravitational force and the distance between them is inversely proportional to the square of their separation.
- We can make the proportionality an equality by the introduction of a constant.
- The introduction of a minus sign informs us that the gravitational force is attractive.

### Vector Addition

*Source slide 9.*

- Force is a [[Glossary#Vector|vector]] and therefore when we have multiple objects we will be using vector addition in one or two dimensions to resolve the direction and magnitude of force.
- A experiences a force from B and C in the same direction.
- Therefore, we add the forces together for the resultant force.
- Vector addition is simply putting each tail on a bum

### Vector addition in 1 dimension

*Source slide 10.*

- To add vectors we place them bum to tail.
- The start of the first bum to the last tail is the resultant.

### Measuring Angles

*Source slide 16.*

- To find the angle the resultant vector is acting in we use a protractor.
- We use the scale on the outside.
- We always place the protractor at 0.
- The start of the vector is placed on the T mark.

### Vectors in 2 Dimensions

*Source slide 21.*

- The same process applies in two dimensions but we will need to either use Pythagoras’ theorem, or the sine or cosine rule if the angle is not 90o.
- Step one: Determine the individual forces
- Step two: Use vector addition

## Equations

### Newton's law of gravitation

$$F=\frac{GMm}{r^2}$$

Magnitude of the attractive force between spherical/point masses.

See [[Formula Sheet]] for the complete course formula set.

## Method

1. Measure \(r\) between the centres of spherical masses.
2. State that the force acts along the line joining the centres and is attractive.

## Common mistakes

- The inverse-square distance is centre-to-centre separation, not the gap between surfaces.
