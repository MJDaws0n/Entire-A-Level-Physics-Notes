### Stable Orbits for Satellites

*Source slides 9–11.*

- The gravitational [[Glossary#Force|force]] is attractive.
- A stationary satellite, with respect to the centre of the Earth, would simply fall towards the centre.
- Isaac Newton imagined firing a cannon ball from a tall mountain.
- He concluded that if you could fire it at the right speed, it could orbit the Earth.
- Satellites orbiting the Earth obey Kepler’s laws of planetary motion.
- We can model their orbits as a circle.
- M is the mass of the Earth

## Equations

### Newton's law of gravitation

$$F=\frac{GMm}{r^2}$$

Magnitude of the attractive force between spherical/point masses.

### Centripetal acceleration and force

$$a_c=\frac{v^2}{r}=\omega^2r$$

Centripetal acceleration.

$$F_c=\frac{mv^2}{r}=m\omega^2r$$

Resultant inward force.

### Kepler's laws and circular orbits

$$T^2=\frac{4\pi^2}{GM}r^3$$

Circular orbit/Kepler's third law.

$$v=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}$$

Speed in a circular gravitational orbit.

See [[Formula Sheet]] for the complete course formula set.

## Method

1. Measure \(r\) between the centres of spherical masses.
2. State that the force acts along the line joining the centres and is attractive.
3. Identify the real forces, then set their inward resultant equal to \(mv^2/r\).
4. Centripetal force is a role played by real forces, not an extra force.
5. For a circular orbit, set gravitational force equal to the required centripetal force.
6. Use orbital radius from the central body's centre, including its radius when altitude is given.

## Common mistakes

- The inverse-square distance is centre-to-centre separation, not the gap between surfaces.
- Velocity is tangential while acceleration and resultant force point toward the centre.
- A satellite in circular orbit is continuously falling; it is not outside the gravitational field.
