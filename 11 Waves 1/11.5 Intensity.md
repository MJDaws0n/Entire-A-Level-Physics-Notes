## Core notes

### Intensity and Supernova

*Source slide 5.*

- The further you travel from a light source the less concentrated the [[Glossary#Energy|energy]] of the wave and thus the dimmer the source looks.
- As certain stars at the end of their lives go supernova and the initial brightness of this phenomena is known we can use this fact to determine astronomical distances.

### Intensity

*Source slides 6–8.*

- The [[Glossary#Intensity|intensity]] of a [[Glossary#Progressive Wave|progressive wave]] is defined by the radiant [[Glossary#Power|power]] through a surface with unit area.
- Extension: State intensity in base units.
- We can show that intensity decreases when we move away from the source.

### Intensity and Distance

*Source slide 9.*

- As intensity decreases with distance we need a way to mathematically describe this.
- For a point source with uniformed power distribution we can use concentric spheres to determine intensity at various distances.
- This is an inverse square law.

### Example: Intensity

*Source slide 10.*

- An LED rated at 2.00 W is placed at one meter intervals away from a lumen detector.

### Example: Sun and Earth

*Source slide 11.*

- The average distance from the Earth and Sun is 150 million km.
- The intensity of radiation received in the upper atmosphere is 1400 Wm-2.
- Step Two: Equation and substitute
- Step one: State what you know

### Intensity and Amplitude

*Source slide 12.*

- As the [[Glossary#Amplitude|amplitude]] can be positive and negative, due to it being a [[Glossary#Displacement|displacement]].
- The intensity is related to the square of the amplitude.
- As intensity (Wm-2) is related to energy which cannot be negative as energy is a [[Glossary#Scalar|scalar]] quantity.

### Intensity and LDRs

*Source slide 13.*

- We can use an LDR to investigate how intensity varies with distance from a constant power source.
- In order to determine the intensity a calibration curve is used.
- Each LDR has its own calibration curve that allows the user to convert the [[Glossary#Resistance|resistance]] of the LDR into light intensity.

## Equations

### Wave intensity

$$I=\frac{P}{A}$$

Power per unit area.

$$I=\frac{P}{4\pi r^2}$$

Intensity from an isotropic point source.

$$I\propto A^2$$

Intensity is proportional to amplitude squared.

### Progressive waves

$$v=f\lambda$$

Wave speed.

$$f=\frac{1}{T}$$

Frequency and period.

$$\Delta\phi=2\pi\frac{\Delta x}{\lambda}=2\pi\frac{\Delta t}{T}$$

Phase difference in radians.

See [[Formula Sheet]] for the complete course formula set.

## Method

1. Use the area over which the power is spread.
2. For an inverse-square comparison, form a ratio before substituting.
3. Measure wavelength between neighbouring points in the same phase.
4. Keep particle motion separate from the direction of energy transfer.
5. Use peak amplitude, not peak-to-peak displacement, unless instructed otherwise.

## Common mistakes

- Doubling distance quarters intensity for an isotropic point source.
- Wave speed is not the speed of oscillating particles in the medium.
