### Diamonds are a Physicist’s best friend

*Source slide 6.*

- A diamond is a transparent crystal of bonded carbon atoms in a covalent network lattice.
- Diamonds have been adapted for many uses because of the material's exceptional physical characteristics.
- These properties determine the major industrial application of diamond in cutting and polishing tools and the scientific applications in diamond knives and diamond anvil cells.

### Cartels and Diamond production

*Source slide 7.*

- It may be surprising but diamonds are both plentiful and relatively easy to manufacture.
- The reasons diamonds have such a high price is because the supply of diamonds is strictly controlled in a monopoly (cartel) structure and through manipulation of markets through very successful marketing campaigns in the 1930’s with the tag line, ‘diamonds are forever’.

### Shiny Objects

*Source slide 9.*

- People like diamonds mainly because they have been sold an effective marketing campaign and they are shiny.
- Their shininess is due to their high [[Glossary#Refractive Index|refractive index]].
- This means that when a light ray enters the crystal lattice structure it is reflected of the boundaries of the crystal several times before the ray can leave.

### Total Internal Reflection

*Source slide 10.*

- When a wave is incident upon a boundary it is normally partially reflected and partially refracted.
- [[Glossary#Total Internal Reflection|Total internal reflection]] is when all the wave is reflected.

### Total Internal Reflection and Conditions

*Source slide 11.*

- The light must be traveling through a medium with a higher refractive index as it hits a boundary with a material with a lower refractive index.
- The angle at which the light strikes the boundary must be above the [[Glossary#Critical Angle|critical angle]] which depends upon the refractive index of the material.

### Determining the critical angle

*Source slide 14.*

- The critical angle must be when the refracted angle equals 90°.

### Optical Fibres

*Source slides 16–17.*

- Optical fibres are designed to totally internally reflect pulses of visible light travelling through them.
- They have many uses including transferring data for broadband connections and imagining during keyhole surgeries.
- A simple optical fibre has a fine glass core surrounded by a glass cladding with a lower refractive index.
- Light travelling though the fibre is contained within the core because of total internal reflection at the boundary.
- Simple optical fibres are step indexed.
- The refractive index changes suddenly between the core and the cladding.

## Equations

### Refraction and total internal reflection

$$n=\frac{c}{v}$$

Absolute refractive index.

$$n_1\sin\theta_1=n_2\sin\theta_2$$

Snell's law.

$$\sin C=\frac{n_2}{n_1}$$

Critical angle from higher index \(n_1\) to lower index \(n_2\).

See [[Formula Sheet]] for the complete course formula set.

## Method

1. Measure every angle from the normal.
2. On entering a higher refractive index, a ray slows and bends towards the normal.
3. Total internal reflection requires travel from higher to lower refractive index and incidence greater than the critical angle.

## Common mistakes

- Frequency remains constant at a boundary; speed and wavelength change.
