## Core notes

### 20.5 The Evolution of the Universe

*Source slide 1.*

- The two pieces of evidence which support the Big-bang model are Hubble’s law (expanding Universe) and the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB).
- The early observable Universe was extremely hot and dense; expansion occurred everywhere, not from a point in pre-existing space.
- As the Universe expanded, it cooled.

### Evolution of the Universe

*Source slide 3.*

- You need to be able to describe the evolution of the universe after the Big Bang to the present day.
- The best current estimate is about 13.8 billion years.

### The Composition of the Universe

*Source slides 10–11.*

- When we count all the matter ([[Glossary#Energy|energy]]) that we can observe in the Universe it cannot account for the experimental results that we observe.
- Galaxies have orbital velocities that are greater than they should with the amount of matter we can observe.
- The rate of expansion of the Universe is increasing.
- To account for the discrepancy between our observations cosmologist suggest that there are two additional quantities in the Universe – dark matter and dark energy.
- Ordinary baryonic matter contributes about 5% of the Universe's total mass-energy content.

### Dark Matter

*Source slides 12–14.*

- We expected that galaxies would act like the planets in the solar system.
- As distance increases from the centre the orbital speed decreases.
- The orbital speed decreases because the gravitational [[Glossary#Force|force]] has decreased.
- This is not the case for galaxies.
- In the 1970s, astronomers studying Doppler shifts in light from galaxies found that the orbital velocities did not decreases with distance.
- Therefore, it was proposed that there was some additional matter (mass) on the outside of the galaxies increasing the star’s velocities.

### Observation and Predicted Theory

*Source slide 15.*

- Distance from centre of galaxy

### Additional Beyond A-level

*Source slides 18–25.*

- In short, no one knows.
- It is said to be dark meaning it doesn’t interact with light.
- It is said to be matter therefore it should have mass.
- There are many candidates, such as weakly interacting massive particles (wimps), axions, black holes and many more.
- Dark matter is inferred from gravitational effects; its particle nature is not established in this course.
- Cosmology is a hard science to make progress with.
- You make observations from telescopes.

### Dark Energy

*Source slides 19–20.*

- Hubble explained that the Universe was expanding yet it was expected to be constant expansion, or the rate of expansion would be slowing down over time.
- Dark energy is the name given to the component used in cosmological models to account for accelerated expansion.
- In 1999 Saul Perlmutter, Brain Schmidt, and Adam Riess were investigating light from a type 1a supernova.
- Type Ia supernovae have standardisable intrinsic luminosities and characteristic light curves.
- Therefore, these supernova are useful in measuring distances in the Universe.
- The further away the supernova the lower the [[Glossary#Intensity|intensity]] on Earth.
- The supernovae appeared dimmer than expected, providing evidence that cosmic expansion is accelerating.

## Method

1. Connect each conclusion to evidence: galactic redshift, cosmic microwave background radiation, and primordial light-element abundances.
2. Describe expansion as increasing scale between unbound galaxies, not an explosion from a point into pre-existing space.
3. Convert megaparsecs to metres before using \(H_0^{-1}\) in seconds.

## Common mistakes

- The observable Universe has no identified central expansion point.
- Hubble's law describes large-scale cosmic expansion, not ordinary motion through local space.
