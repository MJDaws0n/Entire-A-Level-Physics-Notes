## Core notes

### 27.4 The Gamma Camera

*Source slide 1.*

- CAT scans produce a 3-D image that allows doctors to find the shape, size and position of issues like tumours.
- CAT scans can differentiate between soft tissues with similar attenuation coefficients.

### Medical Applications of radionuclides

*Source slide 3.*

- Radioactive contamination has medical applications.
- A doctor may inject a patient with a radioactive substance called a tracer that emits gamma radiation.
- The radioactive substance is absorbed by organs in the body.
- The gamma radiation is detected with a gamma camera.
- The camera makes images of your organs that allows the doctor to diagnose disease.

### Radiopharmaceuticals

*Source slide 4.*

- Radiopharmaceuticals are drugs that contain radioactive forms of chemical elements called radioisotopes.
- A patient is injected (contaminated) with a radioisotope, called a tracer, that emits gamma photons that can be viewed with a gamma camera.

### Tracers - Penetration Power

*Source slides 5–6.*

- A doctor will choose a radioactive tracer to inject into a patient that emits gamma radiation.
- This is due to the penetration [[Glossary#Power|power]] of gamma radiation.
- We want to minimise the amount of radiation absorbed by the patient.
- This is why a doctor would choose a tracer that emits gamma radiation.
- Alpha radiation would be absorbed by the patient due to a low penetration power.

### Tracers and Half-life

*Source slide 7.*

- The doctor will not select a radioactive tracer with a long [[Glossary#Half-Life|half-life]].
- As the patient would absorb more radiation.
- The doctor will not select a radioactive tracer with a very short half-life.
- As there wouldn’t be enough time to capture an image.

### Radioisotopes

*Source slide 9.*

- The radioisotopes used in medicine are produced artificially.
- Fluorine-18 is used for PET scans (Lesson 27.5).
- Technetium-99m (Tc-99m) can be used in various imaging techniques to monitor the function of major organs such as the heart, liver, lungs, kidneys, and brain.
- The radiopharmaceuticals need to be chemically combined with other elements that will target cells in the organs that want to be imaged.
- The concentration of the radiopharmaceuticals allows doctors to identify irregularities in the function of the organs.

### Technetium-99m Decay Route

*Source slide 10.*

- The m refers to the product being meta-stable.
- After a half-life of 6 hours, Technetium-99m decays via gamma emission to Technetium-99.

### Technetium-99m

*Source slide 12.*

- Tc-99m is in a high-[[Glossary#Energy|energy]] state.
- The nucleus has excess energy.
- This photon can then be detected with a gamma camera.

### Collimator

*Source slide 14.*

- A collimator is a device that changes diverging light or other radiation from a point source into a parallel beam.

### Scintillator

*Source slide 15.*

- A scintillator is a material that emits light (scintillates) when struck by ionising radiation.
- The scintillator absorbs around 10 % of the gamma photons and produces photons of visible light.
- This conversion is crucial because gamma rays are difficult to detect directly.
- The scintillator material is typically thallium-activated sodium iodide.

### Photomultiplier tubes

*Source slide 16.*

- The visible light then travels through a light guide to a photomultiplier tube.
- The photomultiplier tube uses the photoelectric effect to produce an electron.
- The electron is accelerated through a [[Glossary#Potential Difference|potential difference]] to hit a target.
- This increases the number of electrons and produces an electrical signal.

### Computer

*Source slide 17.*

- The electrical signal is then processed by a computer to produce an image.
- The image shows the functioning of the organ and not necessarily the anatomy.
- For example, if one of the kidneys has not absorbed the radioactive tracer, as it is not functioning correctly, the image of the gamma camera will demonstrate this.

## Equations

### Nuclear medical imaging

$$A=\lambda N$$

Tracer activity.

$$N=N_0e^{-\lambda t}$$

Tracer decay.

$$e^-+e^+\rightarrow2\gamma$$

Positron-electron annihilation; photons travel approximately opposite directions.

### Radioactive decay

$$\frac{\Delta N}{\Delta t}=-\lambda N$$

Decay rate.

$$N=N_0e^{-\lambda t},\qquad A=A_0e^{-\lambda t}$$

Exponential decay.

$$\lambda t_{1/2}=\ln 2$$

Half-life relationship.

## Method

1. Choose a tracer with suitable chemistry, gamma energy and half-life.
2. A gamma camera uses a collimator, scintillator, photomultipliers and position/energy processing.
3. PET locates near-simultaneous annihilation photons detected in coincidence.
4. Subtract background count rate before analysing source data.
5. Use repeated half-lives for simple factors of two; otherwise use the exponential equation.
6. A plot of \(\ln A\) or \(\ln N\) against \(t\) has gradient \(-\lambda\).

## Common mistakes

- The gamma-camera collimator provides direction information but absorbs many photons, reducing sensitivity.
- Decay is random for one nucleus but statistically predictable for a large sample.
- Activity is measured in becquerels; count rate depends on detector efficiency and geometry.
