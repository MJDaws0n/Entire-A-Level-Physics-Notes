## Core notes

### Scientific Diagrams

*Source slide 3.*

- Waves are oscillations that transfer [[Glossary#Energy|energy]].
- We can depict (draw) waves in different ways.
- Each way has its own strength and weakness.
- The simplest way to draw a wave is with a ray.
- The arrow points in the direction of the energy transport.

### Ultrasound

*Source slide 5.*

- Ultrasound is sound with a [[Glossary#Frequency|frequency]] greater than 20 000 Hz
- The ultrasound beam is transmitted from a transducer.
- The wave is transmitted, absorbed, and reflected at different boundaries.
- The reflections received by the transducer allow a computer to produce an image.

### The Transducer

*Source slide 7.*

- A transducer can generate and receive ultrasound signals.
- It changes electrical energy into sound and sound into electrical energy.
- The transducer achieves this through the piezoelectric effect.

### The Piezoelectric Effect

*Source slides 8–9.*

- Some crystals, like quartz, produce an emf ([[Glossary#Electromotive Force|electromotive force]]) when forces are applied to them.
- The piezoelectric effect is reversible.
- When an external p.d. is applied across a crystal, the electric field compresses or stretches the crystal.

### Transducer – Producing Ultrasound

*Source slide 10.*

- To generate ultrasound, a high frequency (5 MHz) alternating pd is applied across the crystal.
- The crystal quickly compresses and expands.
- The frequency is chosen to match the natural frequency of the crystal.
- This results in crystal [[Glossary#Resonance|resonance]] and produces an intense pulsing ultrasound signal.

### Transducer – Detecting Ultrasound

*Source slide 11.*

- The same transducer is used to detect ultrasound.
- The ultrasound is incident upon the transducer.
- This causes the crystal to be compressed and expanded by small amounts.
- This compression and expansion produce an induced emf across the crystal that can be detected.

### A-Scans

*Source slide 13.*

- A-scans are the simplest ultrasound scan.
- A transducer emits pulses of ultrasound in a straight line which is incident upon a patient.
- A different boundaries, the pulse is transmitted or reflected.
- The reflected ultrasound is then incident upon the transducer with a lower [[Glossary#Intensity|intensity]].
- If the average speed of the sound wave in the medium is known, you can calculate the distances to the boundaries.

### B-Scans

*Source slide 15.*

- B-scans produce a two-dimensional image.
- The transducer is moved across the patient’s skin.
- At each point, the transducer receives a reflection which produces a row of dots on a computer screen.
- These dots correspond to different boundaries.
- The intensity of the dots at each point is then combined to produce a 2-D image.

## Equations

### Ultrasound pulse-echo imaging

$$v=f\lambda$$

Wave speed.

$$d=\frac{ct}{2}$$

Pulse-echo depth.

### Progressive waves

$$f=\frac{1}{T}$$

Frequency and period.

$$\Delta\phi=2\pi\frac{\Delta x}{\lambda}=2\pi\frac{\Delta t}{T}$$

Phase difference in radians.

## Method

1. Use half the round-trip distance in pulse-echo calculations.
2. Coupling gel removes the air gap and reduces the impedance mismatch at the skin.
3. Measure wavelength between neighbouring points in the same phase.
4. Keep particle motion separate from the direction of energy transfer.
5. Use peak amplitude, not peak-to-peak displacement, unless instructed otherwise.

## Common mistakes

- Do not forget the pulse travels to the boundary and back.
- Wave speed is not the speed of oscillating particles in the medium.
