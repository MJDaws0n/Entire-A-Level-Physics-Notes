## Core notes

### Computerised Tomography (CT)

*Source slide 3.*

- A computerised tomography (CT) scan is a type of X-ray scan.
- CT scans around a patient to generate a three-dimensional (3D) image.
- For CT scans, the different rates of absorption (attenuation) of the X-rays allows the computer to produce the image.
- A contrast medium is sometimes used to improve the image quality.

### CAT Scan

*Source slide 4.*

- CT scans and CAT scans are used interchangeably in the medical community.
- CT stands for computerised tomography.
- CAT stands for computerised axial tomography.
- Computerised means with the use of computers.
- Axial means along the centre of a round body, or the axis of rotation of a body.

### CAT Scanner

*Source slide 6.*

- An X-ray tube produces a beam of X-rays.
- The X-ray beam irradiates a thin slice of the patient.
- The X-rays are attenuated by different amounts by the diverse types of tissue.
- The [[Glossary#Intensity|intensity]] of the transmitted X-rays is recorded by the detectors, which produces an electrical input for the computers.
- The X-ray tube and detector rotate 360o producing different slices of the patient.

### Advantages and Disadvantages

*Source slide 7.*

- A traditional X-ray is quicker and cheaper than a CAT scan.
- CAT scans produce a 3-D image that allows doctors to find the shape, size and position of issues like tumours.
- CAT scans can differentiate between soft tissues with similar attenuation coefficients.
- CAT scans exposure time to ionising radiation is greater than a traditional X-ray.
- This also means that the patient needs to remain still for a longer time.
