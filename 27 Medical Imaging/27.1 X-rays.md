### X-rays

*Source slides 5–8.*

- X-rays were the first electromagnetic imaging techniques used.
- One of the first images he took was of Anna Ludwig’s (his wife’s) hand.
- You could now see inside people without cutting them up!
- X-rays are a form of ionising radiation.
- X-rays can treat and cause cancer.
- Modern medical X-rays are low-[[Glossary#Intensity|intensity]] and are absorbed for a very short time to ensure they are relativity safe.
- X-rays can be polarised when diffracted through a crystal.

### The X-ray Tube

*Source slide 9.*

- 4) When the electrons hit the target at high speed, they lose some of their [[Glossary#Kinetic Energy|kinetic energy]].
- This is emitted as X-ray photons
- 2) Releases electrons through thermionic emission.
- 3) A [[Glossary#Potential Difference|potential difference]] is used to accelerate the electrons.

### X-ray tube components

*Source slide 10.*

- The high-voltage supply accelerates the electrons.
- The higher the potential difference, the greater the speed of the electrons.
- The cathode (negative terminal) is heated to liberate electrons by thermionic emission.
- The anode (positive terminal) is connected to a high voltage (200 kV) to accelerate the electrons.
- Electrons hit the metal target.

### Energy transfers

*Source slide 12.*

- When the high-speed electrons hit the metal target, they undergo a steep change in [[Glossary#Velocity|velocity]] (deceleration).
- When a charged particle decelerates quickly, around 1% of the energy is converted into a photon.
- The rest of the kinetic energy is transferred to a thermal store.
- This means X-ray tubes need to be cooled.
- Thermal store of the X-ray tube

### Increasing current

*Source slide 16.*

- The cathode is heated by the current in the conductor.
- This produces electrons in the vacuum tube by thermionic emission.
- By increasing the current in the cathode, the temperature of the cathode increases.
- This produces more thermionic electrons.
- Increasing the number of thermionic electrons, by increasing the current in the cathode, results in a greater intensity (amount) of X-rays.

## Equations

### X-rays and attenuation

$$E_{\max}=eV=\frac{hc}{\lambda_{\min}}$$

Maximum X-ray photon energy and minimum wavelength.

$$I=I_0e^{-\mu x}$$

Attenuation in a material.

$$x_{1/2}=\frac{\ln2}{\mu}$$

Half-value thickness.

### Accelerating charged particles

$$\Delta E_k=qV$$

Kinetic-energy gain across a potential difference.

$$\frac{1}{2}mv^2=qV$$

Non-relativistic speed after acceleration from rest.

$$1\,\mathrm{eV}=1.60\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}$$

Electronvolt conversion.

### Photons and wave-particle duality

$$E=hf=\frac{hc}{\lambda}$$

Photon energy.

$$p=\frac{h}{\lambda}$$

Photon momentum and de Broglie relationship.

$$\lambda=\frac{h}{p}$$

de Broglie wavelength of a particle.

## Method

1. Distinguish the continuous braking spectrum from characteristic lines.
2. Use a thickness in metres when \(\mu\) is in \(\mathrm{m^{-1}}\).
3. CT combines many attenuation measurements to reconstruct slices.
4. Use the magnitude of charge when finding the energy gained.
5. Convert electronvolts to joules before using SI mechanical equations.
6. Convert wavelength to metres before using \(hc/\lambda\).
7. For a massive non-relativistic particle, find momentum from \(p=mv\).

## Common mistakes

- Increasing tube current mainly increases X-ray intensity; increasing tube voltage also raises maximum photon energy.
- The electronvolt is a unit of energy, not potential difference.
- Intensity changes the number of photons per second; frequency changes the energy of each photon.
