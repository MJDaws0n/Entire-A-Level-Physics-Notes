## Core notes

### Quantum Physics

*Source slide 3.*

- Quantum physics explores how, at very small scales, we use different models to describe electromagnetic radiation and subatomic particles like electrons.
- Max Planck, a German scientist, was pivotal in the development of Quantum Physics.
- He discovered that electromagnetic radiation could only exist in certain values – it appear to come in little packets called quanta.
- This particular model was in opposition to radiation being a continuous wave.
- Einstein called these little packets photons.

### Proportionalities

*Source slide 5.*

- As we decrease the [[Glossary#Wavelength|wavelength]] of photons we are increasing their [[Glossary#Energy|energy]].
- This partially explains why X-rays are damaging to cells in our bodies.

### Small Energies and the Electron Volt

*Source slide 7.*

- At the subatomic scale the SI unit for energy a Joule is huge.
- Therefore, we will introduce a new unit for energy on the quantum scale.
- 1 eV is defined as the energy transferred ([[Glossary#Work Done|work done]]) to move an electron through a [[Glossary#Potential Difference|potential difference]] of 1 V.

### LEDs and Planck’s Constant

*Source slides 9–13.*

- We can conduct a simple experiment with LEDs to determine the value of Planck's constant by considering the energies of the photons which the LED emits.
- Lets look at the I-V characteristic of a typical LED.
- When the pd reaches the threshold pd the LED begins to emit photons at a specific wavelength.
- We can use the voltmeter to measure the minimum potential difference required to turn on the LED.
- If we also know the wavelength (colour) of the photons emitted by the LED we can determine Planck’s constant.
- At the threshold potential difference the energy transferred by an electron to the LED is approximately equal to energy of a single photon which is emitted by the LED.
- We can use this equation to determine the value for Planck’s constant.

## Equations

### Photons and wave-particle duality

$$E=hf=\frac{hc}{\lambda}$$

Photon energy.

$$p=\frac{h}{\lambda}$$

Photon momentum and de Broglie relationship.

$$\lambda=\frac{h}{p}$$

de Broglie wavelength of a particle.

See [[Formula Sheet]] for the complete course formula set.

## Method

1. Convert wavelength to metres before using \(hc/\lambda\).
2. For a massive non-relativistic particle, find momentum from \(p=mv\).

## Common mistakes

- Intensity changes the number of photons per second; frequency changes the energy of each photon.
