## Core notes

### 5.4 Power and Efficiency

*Source slide 1.*

- You are in a roller coaster with your friends.

### Rate of Energy Transfer

*Source slide 3.*

- At times we need [[Glossary#Energy|energy]] quickly and therefore we are concerned with the rate of which energy is transferred.
- This is given by [[Glossary#Power|power]].
- Power is the rate of [[Glossary#Work Done|work done]].

### Power and Motion

*Source slide 5.*

- There are times when a constant [[Glossary#Force|force]] needs to be applied to maintain a constant speed.

### Efficiency and Conservation

*Source slide 6.*

- You should remember the principle of [[Glossary#Conservation Of Energy|conservation of energy]].
- Energy of a closed system is constant.
- Yet this doesn’t mean that processes and machines convert all their energy into useful work.
- In particular thermal loses can mean that the output is much lower than the input.
- Therefore we need to characterise the [[Glossary#Efficiency|efficiency]] of processes.

## Equations

### Power and efficiency

$$P=\frac{W}{t}=\frac{\Delta E}{t}$$

Rate of work or energy transfer.

$$P=Fv$$

Power for force parallel to constant velocity.

$$\eta=\frac{\text{useful output}}{\text{total input}}$$

Efficiency as a decimal; multiply by \(100\%\) for percent.

### Work and mechanical energy

$$W=Fx\cos\theta$$

Work done by a constant force; \(\theta\) is between force and displacement.

$$E_k=\frac{1}{2}mv^2$$

Kinetic energy.

$$\Delta E_p=mg\Delta h$$

Change in gravitational potential energy near Earth's surface.

See [[Formula Sheet]] for the complete course formula set.

## Method

1. Use energy or power consistently in the efficiency ratio.
2. Convert percentages to decimals before using them in calculations.
3. Identify the initial and final energy stores.
4. Include work done against resistive forces as energy transferred to thermal stores.
5. Equate energy changes only after accounting for every transfer.

## Common mistakes

- Efficiency has no unit and cannot exceed 1 or 100%.
- Use vertical height change in \(mg\Delta h\), not distance along a slope.
