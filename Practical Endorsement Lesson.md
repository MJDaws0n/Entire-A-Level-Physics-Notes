## Core notes

### Hazards

*Source slide 2.*

- A hazard is anything that has the potential to cause harm.
- When working in a laboratory, you will encounter different hazards, and you need to be aware of how to minimise risk and work safely to protect yourself and others around you.
- There are many types of hazards.
- Including, biological, chemical, physical, and radiation hazards.

### Chemical Hazards

*Source slides 3–4.*

- You are unlikely to encounter chemical hazards in a physics laboratory at A-level.
- Yet, in a professional physics laboratory, you are likely to use hazardous chemical substances.
- In the UK, chemical hazards are covered by the Control of Substances Hazardous to Health Regulations (COSHH).
- The Health and Safety Executive (HSE) is the UK's national regulator for workplace health and safety.
- Each type of chemical hazard has an associated hazard pictogram (symbol).
- These hazard pictograms have been harmonised across the EU and UK.
- Therefore, if you work in any laboratory within Europe, you should be aware of the hazards and how to minimise the harm.

### Lasers

*Source slide 8.*

- Lasers are commonly used in physics laboratories.
- The light from is classed as non-ionising radiation.
- The use of these light sources may cause a significant risk of serious, irreversible eye or skin damage.
- A class 1 laser poses the lowest risk, class 4 the highest.
- Only class 1 or 2 lasers are used in a classroom.

### Ionising Radiation

*Source slide 10.*

- Radioactive substances are essential tools in many areas of scientific research and are a source of ionising radiation.
- Ionising radiations are used widely in medicine, archaeological investigations, fire protection, and the pharmaceutical industry.
- Justification: showing that the benefits will outweigh any detriment that the radiation might cause.
- Optimisation: keeping all exposures as low as reasonably practicable (ALARP).
- Dose limitation: keeping the doses below specified limits.

### Precautions

*Source slide 11.*

- When conducting experiments with ionising radiation you need to take precautions.
- Keep sources in a sealed lead lined container.

### Background Radiation

*Source slide 12.*

- Our environment contains ionising radiation.
- Background radiation is a measure of the level of ionising radiation present in the environment.
- Radon gas in the air from rocks.

### Radioactive materials

*Source slide 15.*

- Some materials are radioactive.
- Radioactive materials emit ionising radiation.
- Radioactive materials can irradiate or contaminate other materials.
- To contaminate an object you place the radioactive material in or on the object.
- Irradiation is when the radioactive material is outside the body and the ionising radiation travels in to you.

### The Meaning of Contamination

*Source slide 16.*

- To contaminate an object you place the material in or on the object.
- For example, Jerry contaminated the punch with some suspicious yellow liquid.
- Jerry placed the yellow liquid in the punch.
- The food was contaminated when it came into contact with an infected packing material.
- The contaminated object was placed on the object.

### Electrical Safety

*Source slide 17.*

- For electrical circuits, the greater the [[Glossary#Potential Difference|potential difference]], the greater the risk of serious injury or death.
- When using electrical equipment, you should always conduct a visual inspection before using the equipment.
- You should check the leads, plug, and socket before use.
- If you discover an issue, you need to let the lab leader (teacher) know immediately.

### Health and Safety

*Source slide 18.*

- In UK law, health and safety is the responsibility of the employer.
- Employees must cooperate with their employer on health and safety matters.
- The employer must conduct risk assessments.
- The employee must be informed and follow the risk assessment.
- For your practical endorsement, you are required to produce a risk assessment.

### Hierarchy of Control

*Source slide 19.*

- First, consider whether you need to encounter the hazard.
- If it is not necessary, you should eliminate or substitute the hazard.
- If it is necessary to use the hazard, you should as much as possible isolate people from the hazard.
- While ensuring that people in contact with the hazard wear personal protective equipment (PPE).

### Risk Assessment

*Source slide 20.*

- You need to be able to identify hazards and introduce control measures to eliminate or reduce the risk.
- Hazard – is the potential for something to cause harm.
- For example, use of a class 3M laser.
- Risk – is the likelihood (Low, Medium, High or 1 to 5) that the hazard will cause actual harm in the circumstances.
- Control measures – steps or procedures undertaken to reduce or remove the risk of a hazard.

### Significant Figures

*Source slides 34–37.*

- When we measure a quantity, the number of significant figures of the value obtained is defined by the resolution of the measurement device.
- This will give us a certain number of significant figures.
- 4 is one significant figure.
- 3.5 is two significant figures.
- 3.51 is three significant figures.
- With certain numbers, the number of significant figures can be ambiguous.
- Let’s round 228.5 to 2 significant figures.

### Scientific Terminology

*Source slide 39.*

- You are expected to use scientific terminology to describe the results that you determine.

### Accurate and Precise

*Source slide 40.*

- In conducting experiments, we need to ensure that our results are precise and accurate.
- Precision is when there is a small range of results.
- Accurate means being close to the true value.

### Precision and Standard Deviation

*Source slide 41.*

- Precision is the closeness of agreement between measurements obtained under the same conditions.
- Precision is mathematically quantified by the standard deviation.
- A lower standard deviation means data points are clustered closer to the mean, while a higher standard deviation indicates greater spread.
- You will learn about standard deviation in A-level Maths.
- Yet, quantifying standard deviation is beyond the scope of the A-level physics course.

### Repeatability

*Source slide 42.*

- Repeatability is the precision of measurement results produced by one person using the same equipment.

### Reproducibility

*Source slide 43.*

- Reproducibility is the precision obtained when measurement results are produced by different people using equivalent equipment.
- [[Glossary#Acceleration|Acceleration]] due to gravity is 9.81 m s⁻¹2

### Validity

*Source slide 44.*

- Validity can apply to an individual measurement or a whole investigation.
- A measurement is valid if it measures what it is supposed to be measuring.
- An investigative procedure is valid if it is suitable to answer the question being asked.
- A student attempts to measure the mass of a cat and determines it to be 170 N.
- This is not valid as the student has determined the gravitational [[Glossary#Force|force]] experienced by the cat and not its mass.

### Confidence level

*Source slide 45.*

- Confidence is a qualitative judgement expressing the extent to which a conclusion is justified by the quality of the evidence.
- Qualitative means words and not a numerical judgement.
- For example, the confidence level of the conclusion is high/low because of the following reasons…

### Error

*Source slide 46.*

- Error (of measurement) is the difference between an individual measurement and the true value.
- Several students conducted experiments to determine the local value of the [[Glossary#Gravitational Field Strength|gravitational field strength]].
- Student one determined a value of 9.43 m s-2.
- Student two determined a value of 10.67 m s-2.
- The accepted local value is 9.81 m s-2.

### Anomaly

*Source slide 47.*

- An anomaly (outlier) is a value in a set of results that is judged not to be part of the inherent variation.

### Resolution

*Source slide 48.*

- Resolution is the smallest change in the quantity being measured that can be detected by an instrument.
- A standard ruler has a resolution of 1 mm.
- A stopwatch has a resolution of 0.01 s.
- A light gate has a resolution of 0.001 s.

### Uncertainty

*Source slides 49–53.*

- Uncertainty is an estimate attached to a measurement which characterises the range of values within which the true value is asserted to lie.
- Every measurement must have an associated uncertainty attached to it.
- The length of a pencil is 170 ± 1 mm.
- When using any apparatus, you check whether the apparatus itself is marked with the uncertainty.
- The micrometer here has an uncertainty of 0.01 mm.
- The volumetric flask as an uncertainty of 0.08 ml.
- The measurement is 10 ± 0.08 ml.

### Analogue Device Uncertainty

*Source slides 54–56.*

- Half the smallest increment is 0.5 V.
- The uncertainty in measurements is ± 0.5 V.
- Half the smallest increment is 0.25 V.
- The uncertainty in measurements is ± 0.25 V.
- When you measure a distance, you have two points you are measuring.
- The start point and end point.
- Half the smallest increment is 0.5 mm

### Stopwatch Uncertainty

*Source slide 57.*

- A stopwatch measures time with a resolution of 0.01 s.
- A stopwatch is a digital device; you may think the uncertainty would be ± 0.01 s.
- Yet the uncertainty is significantly greater than this.
- A person’s reaction time is roughly between 0.1 – 0.3 s.
- Therefore, the minimum uncertainty related to starting and stopping a stopwatch is ± 0.2 s.

### Significance of Uncertainties

*Source slide 59.*

- The importance of the uncertainty depends on the magnitude of the measurement.
- For example, a ± 0.1 cm uncertainty in a distance measurement of 10 m is negligible.
- While ± 0.1 cm uncertainty in a distance measurement of 0.5 cm is significant.
- We determine the significance of the uncertainty of a measurement with the percentage uncertainty.
- The absolute uncertainty is expressed as ± value.

### Combining Uncertainties

*Source slides 63–71.*

- The uncertainty is calculated from the range of the results.
- The range is 0.04 mm.
- For repeated measurements, the absolute uncertainty is given as half of the range of the values.
- When we want to calculate a quantity from two or more measurements we have obtained, we will need to combine the uncertainties.
- A’s uncertainty is ± 0.1.
- B’s uncertainty is ± 0.2
- Z’s uncertainty is ± 0.3

*Source slide 76.*

- Independent Variable – What we change
- Dependant Variable – What we measure

- The piece of metal for the electromagnet

## Equations

### Practical analysis

$$\text{percentage uncertainty}=\frac{\text{absolute uncertainty}}{\text{measured value}}\times100\%$$

Single measurement.

$$\Delta y=\frac{y_{\max}-y_{\min}}{2}$$

Half-range estimate for repeated readings.

$$m=\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$$

Gradient.

$$y=mx+c$$

Straight-line model.

### SI units, prefixes and conversions

$$Q_{\mathrm{base}} = Q_{\mathrm{prefixed}}\times 10^n$$

Replace the prefix by its power of ten.

$$T\,(\mathrm{K}) = \theta\,({}^{\circ}\mathrm{C}) + 273.15$$

Convert Celsius temperature to kelvin.

### Motion graphs

$$\text{gradient of }s\text{-}t=v$$

Velocity is the displacement-time gradient.

$$\text{gradient of }v\text{-}t=a$$

Acceleration is the velocity-time gradient.

$$\Delta s=\int v\,dt$$

Displacement is signed area under a velocity-time graph.

See [[Formula Sheet]] for the complete course formula set.

## Method

1. Identify independent, dependent and controlled variables.
2. Take repeated readings, record raw data with units, and keep measurement precision consistent.
3. Plot a large graph, draw a best-fit line/curve, and use a large triangle for the gradient.
4. Evaluate limitations by linking each problem to its effect and a realistic improvement.
5. Write the prefix as a power of ten before doing the calculation.
6. Convert every value to coherent SI units, then substitute.
7. Carry extra calculator digits and round only the final answer.
8. For an instantaneous gradient, draw a tangent at the required point.
9. For area, divide the region into simple shapes or count signed squares.
10. Area below the time axis is negative displacement.

## Common mistakes

- Repeatability does not remove a systematic error.
- A percentage difference from an accepted value is not automatically the experimental uncertainty.
- A capital letter can change a prefix: \(\mathrm{m}\) is milli but \(\mathrm{M}\) is mega.
- For squared or cubed units, the conversion factor must also be squared or cubed.
- The area under a displacement-time graph is not a standard motion quantity.
- A horizontal line on a velocity-time graph means constant velocity, not rest unless the line is at zero.
