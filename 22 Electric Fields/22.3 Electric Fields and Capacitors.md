## Core notes

### Liquid Crystal Displays (LCDs)

*Source slides 6–8.*

- LCD screens can be found in calculators and digital clocks.
- LCD technology gained a massive market share in the television market in the early 21st century taking over from cathode-ray tube technology.
- We have seen previously that a capacitor separates electric charge.
- Charged particles create electric field lines.
- We have uniformed electric field lines between the (middle section of the) two plates.
- LCDs contain a material that aligns with the electric field.
- When the liquid crystals are aligned, they allow light to be transmitted.

### Electric Field Between Two Parallel Plates

### Parallel Plate Capacitor's Capacitance

*Source slide 20.*

- Capacitors are simply two metal plates
- This insulator could be air, paper, ceramic or mica.
- The insulator is often called a dielectric material.
- Dielectric materials will increase the relative permittivity of the capacitor.

## Equations

### Uniform electric fields

$$E=\frac{V}{d}$$

Uniform field between parallel plates.

$$F=qE,\qquad a=\frac{qE}{m}$$

Force and acceleration of a charged particle.

### Capacitance

$$C=\frac{Q}{V}$$

Capacitance.

$$C=\frac{\varepsilon_0A}{d}$$

Parallel plates in vacuum or air, neglecting edge effects.

$$C=4\pi\varepsilon_0R$$

Capacitance of an isolated conducting sphere of radius \(R\).

See [[Formula Sheet]] for the complete course formula set.

## Method

1. For motion between plates, calculate \(E\), then \(F=qE\), then \(a=F/m\).
2. Treat motion parallel and perpendicular to the field independently when required.
3. Use the charge magnitude stored on either plate, not the sum of magnitudes on both plates.
4. Read capacitance from the gradient of a \(Q\)-against-\(V\) graph.

## Common mistakes

- A negative charge accelerates opposite to the electric-field direction.
- Capacitance depends on geometry and dielectric, not on the present values of \(Q\) and \(V\) for an ideal linear capacitor.

> [!note]- Slide 23: Millikan’s Experiment
> - Capacitors have a fundamental place in applications such as interactive screens as well as passive screens found on most televisions but they also have a very important place in the history of physics.
> - A simple capacitor circuit was used to show that electric charge comes in little intervals of e.
> - That is, charge is discrete and not continuous.
> - A particle can only have a multiple of e charge.

> [!note]- Slide 24: Millikan’s Experiment
> - The droplets only became stationary at discrete intervals proving that electric charge is not continuous but discreet.

> [!note]- Slide 25: Millikan’s Experiment
> - The experiment led Millikan to be awarded the Nobel Prize in 1923.
> - At the time of the experiment Millikan had a PhD student called Fletcher who contributed to the work yet was not recognised.
> - Millikan told Fletcher if Fletcher allowed Millikan to take sole ownership of the experiment proving the discrete nature of charge he would let Fletcher take sole ownership of a separate result for his PhD thesis.
> - Fletcher kept this secrete pact to the grave and was subsequently found in Fletcher’s diary.

> [!note]- Slide 26: Millikan's experiment as an example of psychological effects in scientific work
> - It is supposed from some that science is purely objective and scientists just publish their results but the history of Millikan’s experiment shows otherwise.
> - Millikan’s result was around 2% off the currently accepted value.
> - Yet it would take 50 years to close the gap towards the now accepted value.
> - This isn’t because further experiments were not getting the now accepted value but because the scientists found experimental errors to reduce the discrepancy between their results and Millikan’s.
