- A uniformly charged sphere can be treated as a point charge.

### Electric Potential Energy

*Source slide 6.*

- The total [[Glossary#Work Done|work done]] is equal to the [[Glossary#Electric Potential|electric potential]] [[Glossary#Energy|energy]].

### Negative energies

*Source slides 10–11.*

- In the previous worked example, we obtained a negative energy as an answer.
- When one of the particles has a negative charge, the answer will also be negative.
- The force between the particles will be attractive
- (force will be negative reducing the distance of separation).
- The energy at infinity will be zero and we require an external source of energy to separate the charges.
- This is the same with gravitational energies we looked at in Chapiter 18.

### Electric Potential Difference

*Source slide 15.*

## Equations

### Electric potential and potential energy

$$V=\frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r}$$

Potential of a point charge.

$$E_p=qV=\frac{Qq}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r}$$

Electric potential energy.

### Coulomb's law and radial fields

$$F=\frac{Qq}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}$$

Coulomb force in vacuum.

$$E=\frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}$$

Radial field of a point charge.

See [[Formula Sheet]] for the complete course formula set.

## Method

1. Add potentials from several source charges as scalars, including signs.
2. Use \(\Delta E_p=q\Delta V\) and energy conservation for particle motion.
3. Measure separation between the charge centres.
4. Find magnitude first, then determine attraction/repulsion and direction from charge signs.

## Common mistakes

- Electric potential can be positive or negative; potential difference is the change between points.
- Field magnitude follows an inverse-square relationship.
