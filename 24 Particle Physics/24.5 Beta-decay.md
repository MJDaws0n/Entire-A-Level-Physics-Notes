## Core notes

### 24.5 Beta Decay

*Source slide 1.*

- Leptons and quarks are fundamental.
- Protons and neutrons are baryons.
- Leptons do not experience the strong [[Glossary#Force|force]].
- Mesons are comprised of a quark and anti-quark

### Radioactive Emissions

*Source slide 3.*

- Most atomic nuclei are stable.
- Some nuclei are unstable and may emit radiation.
- Unstable nuclei may emit alpha particles, beta particles, gamma rays, or neutrons.

### Beta-decays

*Source slide 4.*

- A beta-minus particle is a fast-moving electron; a beta-plus particle is a positron.
- There are no electrons in the nucleus so something special occurs.
- In beta-minus decay, a neutron changes into a proton, an electron and an electron antineutrino.

### Neutrinos

*Source slide 7.*

- There are 100 million neutrinos per cubic meter around you, but they rarely interact with their surroundings.
- Neutrinos are leptons with no charge.
- Neutrinos do not interact via the strong or electromagnetic force.
- Neutrinos have a tiny mass - less than a millionth of an electron’s mass.
- The gravitational force is very weak.

### Nuclear notation

*Source slide 11.*

- We can represent elements, their isotopes, and radiation with nuclear notation.
- During a decay, the [[Glossary#Nucleon|nucleon]] number and atomic number are conserved.
- This means charge is conserved.

### Quark transformation

*Source slide 16.*

- For a β- decay a down quark transforms into an up quark.

### β+ decay

*Source slide 17.*

- For a β+ decay an up quark transforms into a down quark.

## Equations

### Particle and nuclear decay equations

$${}^{A}_{Z}X\rightarrow{}^{A-4}_{Z-2}Y+{}^{4}_{2}\alpha$$

Alpha decay.

$$n\rightarrow p+e^-+\bar{\nu}_e$$

Beta-minus decay; quark change \(d\rightarrow u\).

$$p\rightarrow n+e^++\nu_e$$

Beta-plus decay; quark change \(u\rightarrow d\).

$$e^-+e^+\rightarrow2\gamma$$

Electron-positron annihilation.

## Method

1. Balance nucleon number, charge and lepton number.
2. Include the electron antineutrino in beta-minus decay and electron neutrino in beta-plus decay.

## Common mistakes

- A beta particle is created in the weak interaction; it was not already inside the nucleus.

> [!note]- Slide 5: Wolfgang Pauli
> - Wolfgang Pauli studied beta-decays.
> - He understood that if they only consisted of changing a [[Glossary#Hadron|hadron]] (from a neutron to proton) and emitting a [[Glossary#Lepton|lepton]] (electron) they didn’t conserve [[Glossary#Momentum|momentum]].
> - Therefore, Pauli proposed a new particle, the neutrino, to ensure beta-decays obeyed the law of [[Glossary#Conservation Of Momentum|conservation of momentum]].

> [!note]- Slide 6: Wolfgang Pauli
> - Pauli was initially not happy with his solution to the beta-decay problem.
> - The neutrino was, in Pauli’s words, a “desperate remedy”.
> - “I have done a terrible thing,” he said.
> - “I have postulated a particle that cannot be detected.”

> [!note]- Slide 7: Neutrinos
> - Neutrinos interact through the weak nuclear force that is very short range and weak.

> [!note]- Slide 9: Super-Kamiokande
> - Super-Kamiokande is the large water Cherenkov detector.
> - The Super-Kamiokande is operated by an international collaboration of about 150 people and about 40 institutes.
> - 13,000 photo-multipliers are installed on the tank wall.
> - The detector is located at 1,000 m underground.

> [!note]- Slide 10: Student Activity
> - Neutrinos belong to the family called leptons.
> - Neutrinos do not interact with the strong or electromagnetic forces.
> - Neutrinos interact with the weak nuclear and gravitational force.

> [!note]- Slide 11: Nuclear notation
> - Nucleon number (A) = Protons + Neutrons
> - Atomic number (Z) = Protons
