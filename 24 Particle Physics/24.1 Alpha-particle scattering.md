## Core notes

### Scientific Models

*Source slides 3–4.*

- Scientific models improve over time due to new experiments that lead to the discovery of new particles and evidence that cannot be explained by the old model.
- We have improved our model of the solar system.
- The evidence couldn’t be explained with the old model.
- We needed to improve our model.

### Thomson’s Atomic Model

*Source slide 5.*

- In 1897, JJ Thomson conducted experiments and discovered the electron.
- Thomson needed a new model of the atom that included the electron.
- Atoms contain negative electrons.
- Negative electrons are embedded throughout a positive sphere of charge.
- Atoms are overall neutral (no electric charge).

### Rutherford’s Atomic Model

*Source slide 7.*

- The Planetary or Nuclear Model
- Rutherford conducted a new experiment.
- Thomson’s plum-pudding model couldn’t explain the evidence from the experiment.
- Therefore, Rutherford developed a new atomic model.
- Atoms are mostly empty space.

### Microscopic Interactions

*Source slides 17–18.*

- The scattering of the alpha particles from a gold nucleus can be modelled using Coulomb’s law.
- Alpha particles are a helium nucleus with a charge of +2e.
- A gold nucleus has a charge of +79e.
- There is electrostatic repulsion from the charges.
- In a head on interaction between an α particle and a gold nucleus the α particle stops and is deflected through an angle of 180o.
- In Rutherford’s experiment, he knew the [[Glossary#Kinetic Energy|kinetic energy]] of the α particles.
- We can use the concept of the [[Glossary#Conservation Of Energy|conservation of energy]] to calculate the distance of separation between the α particle and the nucleus.

### Radius of the Nucleus

*Source slide 21.*

- We calculated the stopping distance of the α particle to be on the order of 10-14 m.
- A more energetic α particle would have stopped closer to the gold nucleus.
- Therefore, the stopping distance is not the radius of the nucleus.
- We will see in the next lesson that the radius of a nucleus is typically in the order of 10-15 m.

## Method

1. Link most undeflected alpha particles to atoms being mostly empty space.
2. Link rare large deflections to a very small, massive, positively charged nucleus.
3. Use electrostatic repulsion to explain deflection of positive alpha particles.

## Common mistakes

- The experiment did not show electrons orbiting in fixed classical paths.

> [!note]- Slide 9: Rutherford’s alpha-scattering experiment
> - A narrow beam of α particles (helium nuclei) all with the same kinetic [[Glossary#Energy|energy]] were targeted at a thin gold foil that was a couple of atoms thick.
> - The beam of α particles were scattered off the gold foil at various deflected angles.
> - The α particles hit a fluorescent screen producing a tiny flash of light.
> - A microscope is used to count the number of α particles at different deflected angles for each minute.

> [!note]- Slide 11: Experiment observations
> - Most α particles pass straight through the thin gold foil.
> - Only 1 in 2 000 α particles were scattered.
> - Most of these particles experienced very little scattering (small deflected angles).
> - Only 1 in 10 000 α particles were scattered with a deflected angle greater than 90o.

> [!note]- Slide 12: Experiment conclusions
> - Most of the mass is concentrated in a small region called the nucleus.
> - The nucleus has a positive charge as it repelled the few positive α particles that came near it.
