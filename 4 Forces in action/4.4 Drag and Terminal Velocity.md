## Core notes

### 4.4 Drag and Terminal Velocity

*Source slide 1.*

- Both forces oppose the motion of an object.
- [[Glossary#Drag|Drag]] is when an object’s motion is opposed by a fluid (liquid or gas).
- While friction is when an object’s motion is opposed by its surface being in contact with another object.

### The Factors Affecting Drag Force

*Source slide 3.*

- Objects that move through a fluid (liquid or gas) experience the [[Glossary#Force|force]] drag.
- The drag force is due to that object colliding with the particles in that fluid.
- Therefore, increasing the cross-sectional area of an object increases drag on that object.
- Similarly, increasing the object’s speed increases drag on that object.

### Speed can be a drag

*Source slide 4.*

- Modern cars can reach speeds cars in the past could not but in doing so they experience greater drag.
- Modern vehicles are developed to reduce drag as much as possible to improve fuel [[Glossary#Efficiency|efficiency]].

### Terminal Velocity and Forces

*Source slide 5.*

- The [[Glossary#Acceleration|acceleration]] is due to [[Glossary#Gravitational Field Strength|gravitational field strength]], g, and we’ll presume this doesn’t change over time.
- As the speed increases the drag increases.
- This continues until the drag force balances the weight (mg) of the object.
- This is [[Glossary#Terminal Velocity|terminal velocity]].

## Equations

### Drag and terminal velocity

$$F_{\mathrm{drag}}\propto v$$

A possible low-speed model.

$$F_{\mathrm{drag}}\propto v^2$$

A common turbulent/high-speed model.

$$F_{\mathrm{drag}}=W\quad\text{at terminal velocity}$$

Zero resultant force, so zero acceleration.

### Forces and Newton's laws

$$\sum F=ma$$

Resultant force causes acceleration.

$$W=mg$$

Weight in a gravitational field.

See [[Formula Sheet]] for the complete course formula set.

## Method

1. Compare weight and resistive forces at each stage of the fall.
2. Use the changing resultant force to explain the changing acceleration.
3. At terminal velocity, forces balance; the object continues at constant non-zero velocity.
4. Isolate the object and draw only forces acting on it.
5. Choose axes, resolve forces if needed, then write \(\sum F=ma\) along each axis.
6. For equilibrium or constant velocity, set the resultant force to zero.

## Common mistakes

- Terminal velocity does not mean the object has stopped.
- Newton's third-law pair acts on different objects, so the two forces do not cancel on one free-body diagram.
- Mass is measured in kilograms; weight is a force measured in newtons.
