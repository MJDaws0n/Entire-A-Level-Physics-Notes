## Core notes

### Centre of Mass and Geometric Centre

*Source slide 3.*

- Geometric centre means the middle of an object.
- For objects with uniformed [[Glossary#Density|density]] the centre of mass will be in the geometric centre of that object.
- For example, the centre of mass for a 1 m ruler would be at 0.5 m.
- The centre of mass would be higher than the geometric centre because your head is massive.
- Well more massive than your feet.

### You are in the space.

*Source slide 4.*

- You are attempting to fix an issue on the ISS.
- Yet your spanner left your grip and you attempt to push it back to your workstation.
- Yet every time you attempt to push it back you make the spanner spin uncontrollably.
- A [[Glossary#Force|force]] being applied at the objects centre of mass will produce straight line motion without rotation.

### Centre of Mass and Centre of Gravity

*Source slide 5.*

- Imagine you can see the individual atoms and each atom has a mass, m.
- When combined with a [[Glossary#Gravitational Field Strength|gravitational field strength]], g, this will produce a weight, w.
- The resultant gravitational force, W, on the object will act through a point called the centre of gravity, which coincides with the objects centre of mass.

### The Power of Centre of Mass

*Source slide 6.*

- It is much easier to analyse and solve physics problems if we only concern ourselves with the centre of mass and forces acting through that point rather than lots of tiny forces acting on a body.
- This way we can simplify problems.
- For example we can describe complicated movements of a stunt rider with a smooth parabolic path.

### Finding the Centre of Gravity

*Source slides 7–8.*

- A freely suspended object will come to rest with its centre of gravity below the point that object is suspended by.
- For example, this is how a plumb-line works.
- A heavy object, the plumb-bob, is suspended from a piece of string.
- The plumb-bob will produce a vertical line which is inline with the g and the centre of mass.
- If we suspend an other object it will also come to rest inline with its centre of gravity.
- We can use the plumb-bob to then trace a line on that object.
- We can rotate the object and trace the plum-line again.

## Equations

### Moments, couples and equilibrium

$$M=Fx$$

Moment equals force times perpendicular distance from the pivot.

$$\tau=Fd$$

Torque of a couple; \(d\) is the perpendicular separation of the forces.

$$\sum M_{\mathrm{clockwise}}=\sum M_{\mathrm{anticlockwise}}$$

Rotational equilibrium.

### Forces and Newton's laws

$$\sum F=ma$$

Resultant force causes acceleration.

$$W=mg$$

Weight in a gravitational field.

See [[Formula Sheet]] for the complete course formula set.

## Method

1. Choose a pivot that removes as many unknown forces as possible.
2. Use the perpendicular distance from pivot to line of action, not simply the labelled length.
3. For full equilibrium, also require zero resultant force.
4. Isolate the object and draw only forces acting on it.
5. Choose axes, resolve forces if needed, then write \(\sum F=ma\) along each axis.
6. For equilibrium or constant velocity, set the resultant force to zero.

## Common mistakes

- Moment and energy share the unit \(\mathrm{N\,m}\), but a moment is not written in joules.
- Newton's third-law pair acts on different objects, so the two forces do not cancel on one free-body diagram.
- Mass is measured in kilograms; weight is a force measured in newtons.
