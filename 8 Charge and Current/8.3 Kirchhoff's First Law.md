## Core notes

### Conservation Laws

*Source slide 3.*

- Conservation laws are physicists’ most powerful tool in understanding the natural world.
- Kirchhoff's laws express [[Glossary#Conservation Of Charge|conservation of charge]] at junctions and [[Glossary#Conservation Of Energy|conservation of energy]] around loops.

### Hero of the Story: Emmy Noether

*Source slide 4.*

- Noether showed there was a link between symmetries and conservation laws.
- Einstein’s theory of General Relativity wouldn’t have been possible without her contributions.
- “In the judgement of the most competent living mathematicians, Noether was the most significant creative mathematical genius thus far produced since the higher education of women began.“ Einstein
- She couldn’t lecture under her own name for an extended period and generally ill received by universities because she was a woman.

### Large Hadron Collider (LHC)

*Source slides 5–8.*

- The Large [[Glossary#Hadron|Hadron]] Collider (LHC) is the most powerful particle accelerator ever built.
- The accelerator is at CERN, the European Organization for Nuclear Research, on the Franco-Swiss border near Geneva, Switzerland.
- The LHC is a particle accelerator that pushes protons or ions to near the speed of light.
- It consists of a 27-kilometre ring of superconducting magnets with a number of accelerating structures that boost the [[Glossary#Energy|energy]] of the particles along the way.
- Over 10,000 people work at CERN doing many different jobs.
- "Large" refers to its size, approximately 27km in circumference.
- "Hadron" because it accelerates protons or ions, which belong to the group of particles called hadrons.

### Job Opportunities

*Source slide 7.*

- There are a huge number of different jobs available at CERN – including summer placements for students.

### What are conservation laws?

*Source slide 9.*

- A conservation law states that a particular measurable property of an isolated physical system does not change as the system evolves over time.

### Conservation of Electric Charge

*Source slide 11.*

- Conservation of charge states that charge in a closed system cannot change over time.
- Net charge is conserved in a closed system.

### Gustav Kirchhoff

*Source slide 13.*

- German physicist that was born in 1824.
- He collaborated with Robert Bunsen to discover caesium and rubidium.
- He is commemorated by his namesake being used to denote several laws related to his work with electricity.

### Kirchhoff’s First Law

*Source slide 14.*

- Kirchhoff’s first law deals with electric current.
- The sum of currents into a junction is equal to the sum of currents out of that junction.

### Mathematical Form

*Source slide 15.*

- Simply put, current in equals current out.

## Equations

### Kirchhoff's laws

$$\sum I_{\mathrm{in}}=\sum I_{\mathrm{out}}$$

First law: conservation of charge at a junction.

$$\sum \mathcal{E}=\sum V$$

Second law around a complete loop: conservation of energy per unit charge.

### Charge and current

$$\Delta Q=I\Delta t$$

Charge passing a point.

$$Q=Ne$$

Charge is quantised in multiples of elementary charge.

$$I=Anev_d$$

Mean drift velocity in a conductor.

## Method

1. Mark current directions; a negative answer means the true direction is opposite.
2. Apply the junction rule, then write one independent loop equation at a time.
3. Treat a potential rise and potential drop with consistent signs around each loop.
4. Use conventional current direction unless the question explicitly asks for electron flow.
5. In drift-speed calculations, convert number density and cross-sectional area to SI units.

## Common mistakes

- Current is not used up by a component; energy is transferred from each coulomb of charge.
- Electrons drift opposite to conventional current.
