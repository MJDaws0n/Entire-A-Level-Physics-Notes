## Core notes

### Temperature and Colour

*Source slide 3.*

- We intuitively know that the colour of a glowing object indicates the temperature of that object.
- This phenomenon is the same for stars.

### Radiation

*Source slide 4.*

- All objects with non-zero temperature, emit electromagnetic radiation.
- The type of radiation emitted depends upon temperature.

### Temperature and Frequency

*Source slides 6–7.*

- All objects emit electromagnetic radiation.
- As temperature increases the [[Glossary#Frequency|frequency]] of radiation emitted increases.
- People emit infrared radiation.
- An orange-yellow flame is around 1100 °C.

### Black-body Radiation

*Source slide 8.*

- A black body is an idealised object that absorbs all electromagnetic radiation that is incident upon it.
- When in thermal equilibrium, a black body will emit a characteristic distribution of wavelengths due to its temperature.

### Wein’s Displacements Law

*Source slide 11.*

- Wein’s displacements law says that the maximum [[Glossary#Wavelength|wavelength]] is inversely proportional to the temperature of the object emitting the light.

### Models

*Source slide 13.*

- We know that a black body is an idealised object.
- Nevertheless, Wein’s displacements law allows scientists to analyse the light emitted from objects to approximate their temperature.
- This technique can be used to determine the temperature of stars, fires, and people.

### Historical Note: Classical vs Quantum

*Source slide 18.*

- This part is beyond the scope of the course
- Wien’s [[Glossary#Displacement|displacement]] law leads to an unphysical result.
- As we continue to increase temperature, we continue to decrease the wavelength (increasing the frequency).
- Yet this didn’t agree with observations of such bodies.
- This led to the ultraviolet catastrophe which was one of the first major results which showed that classical mechanics was not sufficient to describe the natural world.

## Equations

### Stellar luminosity and flux

$$L=4\pi R^2\sigma T^4$$

Stefan-Boltzmann luminosity.

$$F=\frac{L}{4\pi d^2}$$

Radiant flux/intensity at distance \(d\).

### Black-body radiation and temperature

$$\lambda_{\max}T=b$$

Wien's displacement law.

### Wave intensity

$$I=\frac{P}{A}$$

Power per unit area.

$$I=\frac{P}{4\pi r^2}$$

Intensity from an isotropic point source.

$$I\propto A^2$$

Intensity is proportional to amplitude squared.

See [[Formula Sheet]] for the complete course formula set.

## Method

1. Use Stefan-Boltzmann with absolute temperature and stellar radius.
2. Keep luminosity (total power) distinct from apparent brightness/flux at Earth.
3. Use the peak wavelength of the continuous spectrum to estimate surface temperature.
4. A hotter black body has a shorter peak wavelength and a larger area under its spectrum.
5. Use the area over which the power is spread.
6. For an inverse-square comparison, form a ratio before substituting.

## Common mistakes

- A hotter star is not automatically more luminous; radius also matters.
- The visible colour is a mixture of wavelengths, not simply the peak wavelength.
- Doubling distance quarters intensity for an isotropic point source.
