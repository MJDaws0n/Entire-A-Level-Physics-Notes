## Core notes

### 19.2 Life Cycles of Stars

*Source slide 1.*

- Fusion produces photons.
- The photons produce a radiation [[Glossary#Pressure|pressure]] that acts in the opposite direction to the gravitational [[Glossary#Force|force]].
- The radiation pressure contributes to the star being in equilibrium during the main sequence.

### Different Stars

*Source slide 3.*

- Stars can have a range of masses.
- We compare the mass of stars in reference to the Sun.
- The most massive star we know of is BAT99-98.
- While the Sun is 4.6 billion years old and will not die for another 5 billion years.
- This is the mass of the Sun.

### Life Cycle of a Star

*Source slides 5–7.*

- Stars the mass of the Sun have a different evolution to large stars.
- Large stars have a spectacular end to their life.
- Black hole or Neutron star
- Evolution of a large star
- Evolution of a Sun like star

### Low Mass Stars

*Source slide 10.*

- Low mass stars have a cooler core than more massive stars.
- Therefore, low mass stars remain in their main sequence for much longer.
- However, after billions of years low mass stars will run low on hydrogen in their core.
- At this stage, the star passes into the next stage of their life cycle.

### Red Giants

*Source slides 11–13.*

- Stars between 0.5 M☉ and 10 M☉ evolve into red giants.
- During the main sequence fusion occurs in the star’s core.
- Fusion fuses hydrogen into helium.
- When the hydrogen fuel in the core is depleted fusion can no longer occurs in the core.
- As fusion in core decreases the radiation and gas pressure decreases.
- This means that the gravitational force is the greater force, and the star begins to collapse.
- As the core shrinks, the pressure increases.

### Red Giant

*Source slide 16.*

- The Sun will form a red giant in around 4 to 5 billion years.
- During the red giant phase the Sun will expand and cool until it has engulfed Mercury and Venus.
- The expanded Sun may approach or engulf Earth's present orbit; the exact outcome is model-dependent.

### White dwarfs

*Source slides 17–18.*

- Eventually most of the layers of the red giant will drift into space as a planetary nebula.
- Leaving behind a dense, hot core called a white dwarf.
- White dwarfs have a surface temperature of as much as 30 000 K.
- No fusion reactions take place inside the white dwarf.
- White dwarfs are extremely hot due to [[Glossary#Energy|energy]] created earlier in the star’s evolution.
- A white dwarf is very dense.
- They generally have the mass of the Sun with the volume of the Earth.

### White Dwarfs to Black Holes?

*Source slides 20–21.*

- This is due to a quantum phenomenon called electron degeneracy.
- The Pauli exclusion principle says two electrons cannot exist in the same quantum state.
- This is why in A-level Chemistry you learn that electrons in the same energy state need different spins.
- When the core of a star collapse under the force of gravity the electrons are squeezed closer and closer together.
- This creates a pressure in the core that prevents a gravitational collapse.
- This pressure is called the electron degeneracy pressure.

### Chandrasekhar Limit

*Source slide 22.*

- There is a limit to the amount of electron degeneracy pressure.
- The electron degeneracy pressure is only sufficient to prevent gravitational collapse if the star’s core has a mass of less than 1.44 M☉.
- 1.44 M☉ is the Chandrasekhar limit.

### More Massive Stars

*Source slide 25.*

- Stars with a mass greater that 10 M☉ have hotter cores.
- Nuclear fusion occurs at a faster rate decreasing their life.
- These stars are in the main sequence for millions of years.

### Stars mass > 10 M☉

*Source slide 26.*

- When a star with a mass greater that 10 M☉ has fused most of the hydrogen they begin the fuse helium.
- The temperatures in the core are much greater therefore the nuclei are moving fast enough to overcome the electrostatic repulsion.
- The fusing of helium causes the core to change, and the star begins to expand creating a red supergiant.

### Red Supergiant

*Source slides 27–28.*

- The red supergiant continues to fuse elements together until it reaches iron.
- This creates layers around an iron core.
- The red supergiant eventually has layers of different elements with the heaviest closest to the core.
- At this stage there is an implosion leading to a shockwave that ejects all the core material into space.
- This is called a supernova.

### Nuclear Fusion

*Source slide 31.*

- During nuclear fusion light atoms fuse and forms heavier ones.
- Fusion in stars builds many nuclei up to the iron region.
- Many nuclei heavier than iron form by neutron capture in evolved stars, supernovae and neutron-star mergers.

### Black Hole

*Source slide 32.*

- If the core has a mass greater than 3 M☉ the gravitational collapse continues to compress the core until a black hole is formed.
- At the event horizon, the classical escape speed reaches the speed of light, so no signal can escape.
- The mass of black holes vary greatly.
- Super-massive black holes are thought to be in the centre of galaxies and have a mass of several million M☉.

### Neutron Star

*Source slide 33.*

- If the mass of the core is greater than the Chandrasekhar limit but less than 3 M☉ gravitational collapse continues forming a neutron star.
- Neutron stars contain predominantly neutron-rich matter and are typically about 20 km in diameter.

## Method

1. Use mass as the main factor determining a star's life cycle.
2. Read a Hertzsprung-Russell diagram from luminosity and surface temperature; the temperature axis decreases to the right.
3. Find mass defect using consistent particle or atomic masses.
4. Convert the mass difference to kilograms before using \(c^2\), unless using a stated \(\mathrm{MeV/u}\) conversion.
5. Compare binding energy per nucleon to explain energy release in fission or fusion.

## Common mistakes

- A position on the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram is not a plot of a star's movement through space.
- Binding energy is the energy required to separate a nucleus; an increase in binding energy corresponds to energy released when the nucleus forms.
