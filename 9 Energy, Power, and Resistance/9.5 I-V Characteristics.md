## Core notes

### Temperature and Resistance

*Source slide 3.*

- Last lesson we looked at [[Glossary#Resistance|resistance]] and temperature for metals.
- An application of this knowledge is the tungsten lamp.
- The tungsten lamp was developed in Hungary in 1904 by Alexander Just and Franjo Hanaman.
- Tungsten is a metal that is incredibly useful as it has the highest melting temperature of all the elements at 3 000 °C.

### I-V Characteristics

*Source slide 7.*

- Increasing the [[Glossary#Potential Difference|potential difference]] increases the temperature.
- The increase of temperature decreases the current.
- The gradient has decreased informing us that the resistance has increased.

### I-V Characteristic Curves

*Source slide 9.*

- EMF sources have a polarity.
- We can reverse the polarity of a source of emf.
- This means we can have a negative potential difference.
- For ohmic components, the gradient is constant because the resistance is constant.
- For non-ohmic components, the gradient changes as the resistance is not constant.

### Practical Endorsement

*Source slide 11.*

- For your next practical endorsement activity we will be creating I-V characteristic curves for filament lamps and fixed resistors.

### Measuring Resistance

*Source slide 12.*

- The resistance of a component depends upon the current flowing through that component.
- Therefore, we need a way to change the current in the circuit to see how the resistance for the device changes.
- This set-up doesn’t allow us to change the current in the circuit.
- You would change the total resistance of the circuit.

### The Simple Series Method

*Source slide 13.*

- We can place a variable resistor into the circuit.
- Resistance and current are inversely proportional.
- When the variable resistor is at a minimum value we get maximum current flowing through the component.
- The current decreases as we increase the resistance of the circuit.
- VT – supplied by the [[Glossary#Power|power]] source, cell or battery.

### The Parallel Potentiometer Method

*Source slide 15.*

- The potentiometer is this device.
- We change the position we ‘tap off’ pd from the potentiometer.
- From Kirchhoff’s 2nd law the emf from the power source equals the potential drops per loop.
- We can start with maximum pd through the component.
- Then we can go to the other case where there is no pd through the component.

## Equations

### Current-voltage characteristics

$$R_{\mathrm{static}}=\frac{V}{I}$$

Resistance at one point on a non-linear characteristic.

$$r_{\mathrm{dynamic}}=\frac{\Delta V}{\Delta I}$$

Local/dynamic resistance from a tangent where required.

### Resistance and resistivity

$$R=\frac{V}{I}$$

Resistance at a particular operating point.

$$R=\frac{\rho L}{A}$$

Resistance of a uniform conductor.

See [[Formula Sheet]] for the complete course formula set.

## Method

1. Identify the component and note whether its characteristic is linear and symmetric.
2. For a filament lamp, increasing temperature raises resistance.
3. A diode conducts strongly only in its forward direction after its characteristic begins to rise.
4. For an ohmic conductor, use the gradient of a \(V\)-against-\(I\) graph.
5. In resistivity work, measure \(L\) and diameter carefully and use \(A=\pi d^2/4\).

## Common mistakes

- A curved \(I\)-\(V\) graph does not have one constant resistance.
- The reciprocal gradient is required if the graph plots \(I\) against \(V\).
- Resistivity is a material property; resistance also depends on dimensions.
