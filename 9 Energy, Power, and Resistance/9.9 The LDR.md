### The LDR

*Source slides 3–4.*

- Light dependent resistors (LDR) are components that do just what they say on the tin.
- LDR’s resistance is inversely proportional to light [[Glossary#Intensity|intensity]].
- The greater the intensity the lower the resistance.
- LDR components are made from semi-conducting materials.
- At low light intensity the material does not have many free electrons yet as the intensity increases the number of free electrons increases.
- Uses of LDR: tennis matches (stop play if too dark), street lights, laptops, and mobile phones.

## Equations

### Potential dividers and sensing circuits

$$V_{\mathrm{out}}=\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}V_{\mathrm{in}}$$

Output across \(R_2\) for an unloaded divider.

$$\frac{V_1}{V_2}=\frac{R_1}{R_2}$$

Voltage ratio in a series pair.

### Current-voltage characteristics

$$R_{\mathrm{static}}=\frac{V}{I}$$

Resistance at one point on a non-linear characteristic.

$$r_{\mathrm{dynamic}}=\frac{\Delta V}{\Delta I}$$

Local/dynamic resistance from a tangent where required.

See [[Formula Sheet]] for the complete course formula set.

## Method

1. Mark exactly which component the output voltage is measured across.
2. Work out how the sensor resistance changes, then how its share of the supply voltage changes.
3. If a load is connected, combine it in parallel with the relevant divider resistor before using the ratio.
4. Identify the component and note whether its characteristic is linear and symmetric.
5. For a filament lamp, increasing temperature raises resistance.
6. A diode conducts strongly only in its forward direction after its characteristic begins to rise.

## Common mistakes

- For an NTC thermistor, resistance falls as temperature rises; for an LDR, resistance falls as light intensity rises.
- A curved \(I\)-\(V\) graph does not have one constant resistance.
