## Core notes

### 26.4 Nuclear Fusion

*Source slide 1.*

- Mass is not conserved in nuclear reactions.
- A moderator is used to slow down fast moving neutrons.
- Control rods are used to absorb neutrons to decreases the rate of reaction.
- They produce radioactive waste.

### Nuclear Fusion

*Source slides 3–4.*

- Nuclear fusion is when light nuclei fuse together to form heavier nuclei.
- Each nuclear fusion reaction releases a lot of [[Glossary#Energy|energy]].
- Nuclear fusion is how stars release energy.
- During nuclear fusion light atoms fuse and forms heavier ones.
- Fusion in stars builds many nuclei up to the iron region.
- Many nuclei heavier than iron form by neutron capture in evolved stars, supernovae and neutron-star mergers.

### Benefits of Nuclear Fusion

*Source slide 6.*

- Nuclear fusion could generate four times more energy per kilogram of fuel than nuclear fission.
- Nuclear fusion produces helium as a waste product, and it is not radioactive.

### Nuclear Fusion Reactor Fuel

*Source slide 9.*

- The best candidate as a fuel for a nuclear fusion reactor are isotopes of hydrogen.
- Hydrogen is the most abundant element in the universe.

### Conditions for Nuclear Fusion

*Source slides 11–13.*

- Nuclear fusion is sustained in stars.
- On Earth we have not currently managed to sustain a nuclear fusion reaction.
- Very high [[Glossary#Density|density]] and [[Glossary#Pressure|pressure]]
- The high temperatures lead to high [[Glossary#Kinetic Energy|kinetic energy]].
- The high kinetic energy results in very high speeds.
- High speeds are needed to overcome the repulsion between the nuclei so they can get close enough for the strong nuclear [[Glossary#Force|force]] to be attractive.
- The high density and pressure increases the possibility of suitable collisions.

### Energy Released

*Source slide 15.*

- The difference in [[Glossary#Binding Energy|binding energy]] between product nucleons and reactant nucleons is the energy released.
- Isolated single protons do not have a binding energy.
- The binding energy of deuterium is 1.1 MeV/[[Glossary#Nucleon|nucleon]].
- Therefore, the energy released is 2.2 MeV.

## Equations

### Mass-energy and binding energy

$$\Delta E=\Delta mc^2$$

Energy released from a mass decrease.

$$E_b=\Delta mc^2$$

Nuclear binding energy from mass defect.

$$1\,\mathrm{u}=1.661\times10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}$$

Atomic mass unit conversion.

$$1\,\mathrm{eV}=1.60\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}$$

Electronvolt conversion.

### Nuclear and particle structure

$$R=r_0A^{1/3}$$

Nuclear radius model.

$$A=Z+N$$

Mass number equals proton plus neutron number.

$$Q_{\mathrm{before}}=Q_{\mathrm{after}}$$

Charge conservation in particle reactions.

## Method

1. Find mass defect using consistent particle or atomic masses.
2. Convert the mass difference to kilograms before using \(c^2\), unless using a stated \(\mathrm{MeV/u}\) conversion.
3. Compare binding energy per nucleon to explain energy release in fission or fusion.
4. Balance charge, baryon number and lepton number in particle equations.
5. For nuclear notation, conserve total nucleon number and proton number.
6. Use antiparticles with equal mass and opposite charge and relevant quantum numbers.
7. Use mass as the main factor determining a star's life cycle.
8. Read a Hertzsprung-Russell diagram from luminosity and surface temperature; the temperature axis decreases to the right.

## Common mistakes

- Binding energy is the energy required to separate a nucleus; an increase in binding energy corresponds to energy released when the nucleus forms.
- Atomic number \(Z\) counts protons; mass number \(A\) counts nucleons, not atomic mass in kilograms.
- A position on the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram is not a plot of a star's movement through space.
