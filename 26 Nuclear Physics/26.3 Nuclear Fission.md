### Nuclear Fission

*Source slides 3–7.*

- Nuclear fission is a type of nuclear reaction.
- Mass is not conserved in nuclear reactions.
- Nuclear fission reactions are used in nuclear reactors.
- Nuclear reactors use uranium or plutonium as a fuel.
- Atoms emit radiation when they are unstable.
- Atomic nuclei emit radiation when they are unstable.

### Chain Reactions

*Source slide 9.*

- The additional neutrons can then be absorbed by other large nuclei.
- This causes a chain reaction.
- We can control the rate of reaction with control rods.
- The control rods absorb the neutrons

### Moderators

*Source slide 12.*

- Neutrons are emitted at high speeds in a nuclear fission reaction.
- The neutrons are slowed down by a moderator to become thermal neutrons.
- A moderator is a material that absorbs [[Glossary#Energy|energy]] from the neutrons.

### Benefits of Nuclear Fission Reactors

*Source slide 16.*

- Uranium is an energy dense fuel.
- The energy in 1 g of uranium is equal to 3 000 kg of coal.

### Dangers of Nuclear Fission Reactors

*Source slides 18–19.*

- Incidents at nuclear fission reactors can release radioactive waste into the environment.
- Nuclear fission reactors produce radioactive waste.
- The spent (used) reactor fuel and other radioactive waste materials emits alpha, beta, and gamma radiation.
- Some of the radioactive waste has a [[Glossary#Half-Life|half-life]] of 100 years.
- The radioactive waste needs to be stored safely.

### Radioactive Waste

*Source slides 20–22.*

- The nuclear fission process produces large amounts of radioactive isotopes, and some of the isotopes have rather long half-lives.
- Those isotopes are safe only after ten half-lives, which presents a problem when dealing with the waste products of a fission reactor.
- Nuclear waste is divided into low-level, medium and high-level material, based on the amount of radioactivity being emitted.
- In the United States, low-level wastes are stored at the site of generation or at special storage facilities.
- The wastes are buried and guarded at the sites.
- High-level wastes pose a much larger problem.
- They’re temporarily being stored at the site of generation, with plans to eventually seal the material in glass and then in drums.

## Equations

### Mass-energy and binding energy

$$\Delta E=\Delta mc^2$$

Energy released from a mass decrease.

$$E_b=\Delta mc^2$$

Nuclear binding energy from mass defect.

$$1\,\mathrm{u}=1.661\times10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}$$

Atomic mass unit conversion.

$$1\,\mathrm{eV}=1.60\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}$$

Electronvolt conversion.

### Nuclear and particle structure

$$R=r_0A^{1/3}$$

Nuclear radius model.

$$A=Z+N$$

Mass number equals proton plus neutron number.

$$Q_{\mathrm{before}}=Q_{\mathrm{after}}$$

Charge conservation in particle reactions.

## Method

1. Find mass defect using consistent particle or atomic masses.
2. Convert the mass difference to kilograms before using \(c^2\), unless using a stated \(\mathrm{MeV/u}\) conversion.
3. Compare binding energy per nucleon to explain energy release in fission or fusion.
4. Balance charge, baryon number and lepton number in particle equations.
5. For nuclear notation, conserve total nucleon number and proton number.
6. Use antiparticles with equal mass and opposite charge and relevant quantum numbers.

## Common mistakes

- Binding energy is the energy required to separate a nucleus; an increase in binding energy corresponds to energy released when the nucleus forms.
- Atomic number \(Z\) counts protons; mass number \(A\) counts nucleons, not atomic mass in kilograms.
