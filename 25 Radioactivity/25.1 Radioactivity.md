## Core notes

### Atoms and Ions

*Source slides 3–4.*

- Atoms have the same number of positive protons and negative electrons.
- Atoms have an overall neutral charge.
- An ion is an electrically charged particle.
- Ions have a different number of positive protons and negative electrons.

### Radioactive Decays

*Source slide 5.*

- Most atomic nuclei are stable.
- Some nuclei are unstable and may emit radiation.
- Unstable nuclei may emit alpha particles, beta particles, gamma rays, or neutrons.

### Ionising Radiation

*Source slide 6.*

- Atoms have no overall (net) charge because they have the same number of negative electrons and positive protons.
- Ions are electrically charge because they have a different number of negative electrons and positive protons.
- Atoms can become ionised by ionising radiation.

### Precautions

*Source slide 9.*

- All types of ionising radiation can damage cells forcing them to mutate.
- This can lead to cancer.
- Keep sources in a sealed lead lined container.

### Detecting Radiation

*Source slide 10.*

- A Geiger (GM tube) counter is used for detecting and measuring ionising radiation.
- Alpha, beta, and gamma radiation are forms of ionising radiation.
- The ionising radiation enters the Geiger counter and produces a click.
- Each click is caused by a tiny current produced when the radiation ionises atoms.
- Current is the net flow of charged particles.

### Background Radiation

*Source slide 11.*

- The soil and rocks are natural sources of ionising radiation.
- The ionising radiation produced from rocks and soil produces a background radiation reading on Geiger counters.

### Effects of Electric and Magnetic Fields

*Source slides 14–15.*

- Charged particles will be deflected as they pass through an electric or magnetic field.
- The direction of the deflection will be found using Fleming’s left-hand rule.
- Gamma radiation has no charge and will not be deflected.
- Alpha has a +2e charge.
- Beta has a ±e charge.
- Beta will be deflected more because
- its mass is far smaller than alpha.

### Cloud Chambers

*Source slide 16.*

- A cloud chamber is a device that allows us to trace the path of a charged particle (ion).
- When a cloud chamber is used in conjunction with a uniformed E or B-field we can differentiate the type of particle from their deflected paths.
- This cloud chamber photograph was used to prove the existence of the positron.

## Equations

### Radioactive decay

$$A=\lambda N$$

Activity.

$$\frac{\Delta N}{\Delta t}=-\lambda N$$

Decay rate.

$$N=N_0e^{-\lambda t},\qquad A=A_0e^{-\lambda t}$$

Exponential decay.

$$\lambda t_{1/2}=\ln 2$$

Half-life relationship.

### Particle and nuclear decay equations

$${}^{A}_{Z}X\rightarrow{}^{A-4}_{Z-2}Y+{}^{4}_{2}\alpha$$

Alpha decay.

$$n\rightarrow p+e^-+\bar{\nu}_e$$

Beta-minus decay; quark change \(d\rightarrow u\).

$$p\rightarrow n+e^++\nu_e$$

Beta-plus decay; quark change \(u\rightarrow d\).

$$e^-+e^+\rightarrow2\gamma$$

Electron-positron annihilation.

See [[Formula Sheet]] for the complete course formula set.

## Method

1. Subtract background count rate before analysing source data.
2. Use repeated half-lives for simple factors of two; otherwise use the exponential equation.
3. A plot of \(\ln A\) or \(\ln N\) against \(t\) has gradient \(-\lambda\).
4. Balance nucleon number, charge and lepton number.
5. Include the electron antineutrino in beta-minus decay and electron neutrino in beta-plus decay.

## Common mistakes

- Decay is random for one nucleus but statistically predictable for a large sample.
- Activity is measured in becquerels; count rate depends on detector efficiency and geometry.
- A beta particle is created in the weak interaction; it was not already inside the nucleus.
