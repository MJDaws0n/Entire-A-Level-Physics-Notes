## Core notes

### Random and Spontaneous

*Source slide 3.*

- We cannot predict when a particular nucleus in a sample will decay or which one will decay next.
- Each nucleus within a sample has the same chance of decaying in unit time.
- The presence of other nuclei in the sample
- External factors such as [[Glossary#Pressure|pressure]].

### Half-life

*Source slides 6–20.*

- Unstable nuclei emit radiation until they are stable.
- The [[Glossary#Half-Life|half-life]] is the average time taken for half a radioactive sample to become stable.
- Half the sample decays to become stable every 20 s.
- We cannot predict which atom will be the next to emit radiation, decay, and become stable.
- Radioactive decays are random.

### Half-life Graphs

*Source slide 7.*

- As a radioactive sample emits radiation and decays to become stable the activity will decrease.
- We can plot the activity of a radioactive sample.
- Half-life from a graph

## Equations

### Radioactive decay

$$A=\lambda N$$

Activity.

$$\frac{\Delta N}{\Delta t}=-\lambda N$$

Decay rate.

$$N=N_0e^{-\lambda t},\qquad A=A_0e^{-\lambda t}$$

Exponential decay.

$$\lambda t_{1/2}=\ln 2$$

Half-life relationship.

See [[Formula Sheet]] for the complete course formula set.

## Method

1. Subtract background count rate before analysing source data.
2. Use repeated half-lives for simple factors of two; otherwise use the exponential equation.
3. A plot of \(\ln A\) or \(\ln N\) against \(t\) has gradient \(-\lambda\).

## Common mistakes

- Decay is random for one nucleus but statistically predictable for a large sample.
- Activity is measured in becquerels; count rate depends on detector efficiency and geometry.
